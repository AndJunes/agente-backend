# Product Positioning

## Definition

[S083] (Aha!): "Product positioning is **a strategic exercise that defines where your product or
service fits in the marketplace and why it is better than alternative solutions.** The goal is to
distill **who your audience is, what they need, and how your product can uniquely help.** Product
positioning is the basis of your marketing story."

[S133] (Productside) adds a sharper, more useful framing:

> "Product positioning is **planning for how people in the market will think about your product.**
> And, remember that **your product has a life of its own. The only product positioning that
> counts is what your customers think. If a customer isn't thinking it, your product doesn't
> occupy that position.**"

> **This is the most important idea on this page.** Positioning is not a statement you write; it
> is a state of mind you are trying to produce in other people. The document is an instrument, not
> the outcome. A team can have a beautifully written positioning statement and no position.

## Internal document, external effect

[S083]: "The outcome of product positioning is **an internal document that informs external
messaging** — including how you will communicate product benefits to customers."

> Note the asymmetry: the artefact is internal, the position is external. This is why [S133]'s
> "only what customers think counts" caveat matters — the internal document is a hypothesis about
> the position you want, and whether you hold it is an empirical question.

## The gap positioning is meant to close

[S133] frames the work as movement between two states:

> "Using product positioning strategy will help you understand: **A. Where your product is today.
> B. Where it would be ideally.** Product positioning strategy is what gets your product position
> from A to B."

Its suggested exercise: "in an ideal world, **what would your target market think of your
product?** You may have several target markets. Write down what each of [them would think]."

> The multiple-target-markets point is easy to miss and consequential: positioning may need to be
> stated separately per segment, which in turn constrains how many segments a product can serve
> coherently.

## Why teams lack positioning

[S083] offers a diagnosis rather than an exhortation:

> "You may find yourself in a company where product positioning strategy is **not well-defined.
> Maybe this is because there is unclear ownership or an ever-changing vision.** Dig deeper and
> you may realize that **you can lead a collaborative effort to fine-tune the positioning. Your
> colleagues in sales and marketing will likely be strong advocates of this work** — effective
> positioning makes it possible to craft meaningful messages and create even more value for
> customers."

> Two named causes — **unclear ownership** and **an unstable vision** — and a practical note that
> the PM often has willing allies in sales and marketing. Positioning is one of the few strategy
> artefacts where other functions actively want the PM to do the work.

## Where positioning sits in strategy

[S090] places positioning in the **Foundation** layer of product strategy, alongside vision and
business models: "Define where your product fits in the marketplace and the unique benefits you
provide. **Strong positioning supports your marketing strategy, brand storytelling, and all
content developed for customers.**"

[S095] places it differently — as an input to the product vision, produced after market research
and target-customer identification. See [product-vision.md](product-vision.md).

> These are not contradictory so much as circular: vision informs positioning informs messaging,
> and positioning cannot be written without knowing the target customer, which the vision helps
> define. The corpus does not resolve the sequencing.

## When to define it

[S133] poses the question "When do I define my product positioning strategy?" but the answering
section did not fully extract. [S083] implies it is ongoing rather than one-off, describing it as
something to "fine-tune."

[S090] places it in the exploration phase of setting strategy: "The exploration phase of setting a
product strategy often involves **competitive analysis, vetting different business models, and
creating product positioning. This needs to happen before any detailed product roadmap work
begins.**"

## Positioning versus value proposition

The corpus does not draw this distinction explicitly, and the two are routinely conflated. What
the sources support:

| | Positioning | Value proposition |
|---|---|---|
| **Question answered** | Where do we fit in the market, and why are we better than alternatives? [S083] | What benefits do we deliver, and why choose us over alternatives? [S005] |
| **Frame of reference** | The **market and competitive set** | The **customer and their problem** |
| **Primary output** | An internal document informing messaging [S083] | A statement usable both externally and as an internal compass [S005] |
| **Test of success** | What customers actually think [S133] | 40% "very disappointed" [S005] |

> **They overlap substantially.** The practical difference is emphasis: positioning starts from
> the competitive landscape and asks where you stand in it; a value proposition starts from the
> customer's problem and asks what you deliver. The Crossing the Chasm template in
> [value-proposition.md](value-proposition.md) contains both — a need statement *and* an explicit
> "Unlike [competitor]" clause — which is why that format is often used for both purposes.
>
> **This distinction is a synthesis, not a corpus claim.** Treat it as a working aid.

## Limitations

- **This is the weakest-covered major strategy topic in the corpus.** [S083] and [S133] are both
  partially extracted: the sections on developing a positioning statement, positioning strategies,
  and "how do people perceive your product?" survive only as headings.
- **No positioning statement template extracted**, despite two sources having sections dedicated to
  one. The Crossing the Chasm template in [value-proposition.md](value-proposition.md) is the
  closest usable substitute in this knowledge base.
- **No treatment of repositioning** — when and how to change a position that is already held.
- **No treatment of positioning research** — how to measure what customers currently think, which
  is precisely what [S133] says is the only thing that counts. Perceptual mapping, brand tracking
  and similar methods are absent.
- **[S082] (Product School, *Product Positioning 101*)** covers a six-step process built largely
  on research methods already documented elsewhere in this knowledge base (customer interviews,
  buyer personas, market research and analytics, monitoring reviews, focus groups, competitor
  audience analysis, social listening, customer journey mapping). Those techniques are treated in
  [../02-discovery](../02-discovery/user-research-methods.md) and
  [../03-market-intelligence](../03-market-intelligence/market-research.md) rather than duplicated
  here.
- [S133] carries an ambiguous "Created / Updated: 11/02/20" stamp; its currency cannot be
  determined.

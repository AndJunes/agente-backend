# Product Vision (and how it differs from Mission)

## The central confusion

[S094] is **Marty Cagan (SVPG), 4 August 2020** — the most authoritative single source in the
corpus on this topic. Its whole purpose is to correct a specific, widespread error:

> "Most people I meet, when they show me their 'product vision,' **what they are really showing me
> is their mission statement.** They are **confusing a slogan about their purpose with a product
> vision.**"

### Why it happens
[S094]: "This problem is enabled because so many people fall for the trap of thinking there's a
**simple fill-in-the-blanks framework, canvas or board** that easily gives them what they need."

### The distinction
- **Mission statement:** "there's nothing inherently bad about having a pithy slogan, like
  *'organize the world's information'* to describe your company's mission. **The mission
  statement tries to make clear to anyone that might not know the purpose of the company, what
  that purpose is.**"
- **Product vision:** something that addresses the needs of empowered product teams — see below.

[S094]'s framing of why terminology is not the point:

> "**The issue is not the terminology.** The issue is that empowered product teams have some very
> real needs that have to be addressed, if you want to have true empowerment, and **a team of
> missionaries versus a team of mercenaries. And a mission statement simply doesn't address these
> needs.**"

## The second confusion: per-team visions

[S094] identifies a structural error:

> "Each product team thinks they are supposed to create their own product vision. This of course
> completely misses **one of the key purposes of the product vision, which is to provide the
> common 'north star' so that every product team, no matter what area they are working on,
> understands how their work contributes to the larger whole.**
>
> When each team has their own vision, it's the equivalent of **everyone picking out their own
> star from the sky, and calling it their 'north star' and then heading in their own
> direction.**"

> **The implication for an agent:** if asked to help a single squad "write our product vision,"
> the useful first question is whether a *company-level* product vision already exists. A
> team-level vision that competes with an organizational one defeats the artefact's purpose.

## What a good product vision does

[S094] lists direct purposes:

- "Keeps us **focused on the customer.**"
- "Serves as the **North Star** for the product organization so that we have a common
  understanding of what we are hoping to accomplish together."
- "**Inspires ordinary people to create extraordinary products.**"
- "Shows us **why this work is meaningful. A list of features on a roadmap is not meaningful. How
  you can positively impact the lives of users and customers is meaningful.**"
- "Illustrates **how we plan to leverage relevant industry trends and technologies** that we
  believe can help us solve problems for our customers **in ways that are just now possible.**"

And indirect benefits — these are the ones most often overlooked:

| Indirect benefit | [S094] |
|---|---|
| **Architecture** | "Provides the engineering organization with enough clarity about what's coming in the next several years so they can ensure they have in place **an architecture that can serve the need**" |
| **Team topology** | "The product vision is **a primary driver of the team topology**" |
| **Strategy input** | "The product vision, **combined with the annual company objectives, drives the product strategy**" |
| **Recruiting** | "One of our most powerful recruiting tools for strong product people" |
| **Evangelism** | "One of our most powerful evangelism tools to enlist the necessary help and support of colleagues from all across the company and beyond — ranging from senior executives, to investors, to sales, services and marketing staff" |

[S094]'s summary: "**I don't think I can name a higher-leverage tool for a product
organization.**"

## What a good vision is like

Three properties, from [S094]:

1. **It is a persuasion tool.** "A good product vision is a bit of an art form, as **fundamentally
   it is a persuasion tool.**" And: "**the product vision needs to be persuasive. It needs to
   inspire.**"
2. **It must not be over-specified.** "It's also important to **not be too detailed or
   prescriptive, which runs the risk of product teams confusing the vision with a
   specification.**"
3. **It should be rendered in a compelling medium.** "**Have you ever been inspired by a canvas or
   board? I know I haven't.**" [S094] describes the alternative: "Increasingly that's a video of a
   **visiontype**, which is a special form of prototype. They **dramatize the user experience.
   They show how our customer's lives demonstrably and emotionally improve. They make it
   visceral.**"

> The *visiontype* concept — a prototype whose purpose is persuasion rather than validation — is
> distinctive and appears nowhere else in the corpus.

**The test of a good vision** [S094]: "When done well, the product vision is compelling,
inspiring, and empowering — **leaving the product teams feeling excited to begin the hard work of
making this vision a reality.**"

## When none of this applies

[S094] closes with a deliberately pointed qualifier:

> "Unless of course **you're a feature team organization, in which case I'm afraid none of this
> really matters, because mercenaries don't need to be inspired, just paid.**"

> **This is an argument, not a description.** Cagan's *empowered product team* vs *feature team*
> distinction is central to his body of work, and the corpus contains **no source defining that
> distinction**. Read here: a product vision is load-bearing only where teams are expected to
> decide *how* to achieve outcomes. Where teams are given specifications to implement, the
> artefact does less work. The corpus cannot corroborate the broader claim. Recorded as a gap.

## Other definitions in the corpus

These are weaker sources and are recorded for contrast rather than authority.

[S090] (Aha!): the vision "**represents the core essence of your product and what makes it
unique.** It should be something that everyone in the company deeply understands — the 'why'
behind the product you are all responsible for."

[S095] (Product School) proposes a **six-step process** for creating one: deep market research →
identifying the target customer → developing the value proposition → defining the unique selling
points → setting the vision statement → documenting it in a product vision document.

> **This process sits in tension with [S094].** Cagan explicitly warns against
> "fill-in-the-blanks framework, canvas or board" approaches, and a six-step template is exactly
> that shape. The two are not reconcilable on method: [S094] treats vision as an act of
> persuasive imagination grounded in trends and customer impact; [S095] treats it as an output of
> a research-and-synthesis pipeline. **Prefer [S094] on what a vision is for; [S095] is useful
> only as a list of inputs worth having.**

## Vision statement examples

[S093] (ProdPad, Janna Bastow) collects ten widely cited vision statements:

> Zoom — "To make video communication frictionless" · Shopify — "To make commerce better for
> everyone" · LinkedIn — "To create economic opportunity" · Instagram — "To capture and share the
> world's moments" · Google — "To provide access to the world's information" · Netflix — "To
> become the world's leading [entertainment distribution service]" · Uber — "Evolving the way the
> world moves" · Stripe — "To grow the GDP of the internet" · OpenAI — "To ensure that AGI
> benefits all of humanity"

> **Note what these are.** By [S094]'s own criteria, most of these are **mission statements, not
> product visions** — they are pithy slogans about purpose, exactly what Cagan says people
> mistake for a vision. The corpus therefore contains a live example of the confusion [S094]
> describes: an article titled *"10 Great Product Vision Examples"* whose contents are largely
> corporate mission statements.
>
> They remain useful as examples of **mission** statements. They should not be used as templates
> for a product vision.
> Recorded in [../99-reference/disputed-information.md](../99-reference/disputed-information.md).

## Where vision sits relative to other artefacts

From [S080]'s navigational analogy: the **vision is the destination**; the strategy is the route;
OKRs are the road signs; the roadmap is the map of the route. See
[okrs.md](okrs.md) and [product-strategy.md](product-strategy.md).

[S094] gives the causal link: "The product vision, **combined with the annual company objectives,
drives the product strategy.**"

## Limitations

- **[S094] is an argument by a named authority, not evidence.** Its claims about what visions
  achieve (recruiting, architecture, topology) are asserted from experience.
- **Cagan's underlying model — empowered teams vs feature teams, missionaries vs mercenaries — is
  assumed rather than established** in the corpus. His books (*Inspired*, *Empowered*) are
  referenced by [S140] but not included.
- **No guidance on how long a vision should hold**, when to revise it, or how to tell a vision
  has gone stale.
- **[S095] and [S093] are commercial content** with template CTAs; their substantive contribution
  is limited to inputs and examples respectively.
- The corpus has **no worked example of a full product vision** in Cagan's sense — only slogans.

# Product Strategy

## Definition

[S090] (Aha!): "Product strategy **outlines how a product will support company strategy and
business goals.** Product strategy serves as a guide for the team planning, developing, and
taking a product from concept through to market release."

Its positioning relative to other artefacts: "Product strategy describes **what you want to
achieve and how you plan to get there.** It defines the '**why**' behind the product. Strategy
happens **before** you dive into the detailed work of building your product roadmap and
developing new features — what we call the '**what**,' '**when**,' and '**how**.'"

And the connection to the roadmap: "**Product strategy is made visual through a product
roadmap.**"

[S080] gives a memorable navigational analogy for the whole stack:

> - 📍 "LA is your destination, your **product vision**."
> - 🛣️ "Your route is the **product strategy**. It's how you will get from Toronto to LA."
> - 🚸 "The road signs are your **OKRs** — they measure how far you've gone and how long you have to go."
> - 🗺️ "Your trusty roadmap would be the **product roadmap**... a physical manifestation of the route."

## The questions a strategy answers

[S090] opens with these, and they function as a checklist:

> "**Who are your customers? What problems will your product solve? What opportunities and
> threats do you face? What will you do differently? How will you stand out in a crowded
> market?**"

[S040] gives a compatible but tighter specification of what a valid product strategy must state:

> "the **users and customers** who will benefit from the product, the **needs** the product will
> address, the **business benefits** it will offer, and the **standout features** which will set
> it apart from competing offerings."

## What product strategy is *not*

This is [S090]'s most useful contribution. Teams routinely present one of the following *as* a
strategy:

| Not a strategy | What it actually is [S090] |
|---|---|
| **Vision and mission** | "Foundational components *of* a strategy" |
| **Metrics** | "KPIs give a sense of how you are tracking against product goals, which in turn are a component of your product strategy" |
| **Budget** | "Financial resources **enable (or constrain)** your ability to deliver against the product strategy" |
| **Ideas** | "Creative concepts and requests for enhancements can support innovation **if they align with** the product strategy" |
| **Features** | "Individual features (or even a collection of functionality) are **one way that you might achieve** the product strategy" |
| **Roadmaps** | "Roadmaps **visualize** the product strategy and offer a timeline for implementation" |
| **Launch plans** | "A list of activities that a cross-functional team will complete as part of releasing new functionality" |

### The planning trap
[S090] names the specific failure:

> "Many teams **conflate a product plan with a product strategy.** Because product development is
> dynamic and unpredictable, folks tend to jump straight to pulling together a list of ideas or
> activities that seem to align with a loose set of goals. You believe you can implement that
> list with the resources you have available and are then **lulled into a false sense of
> strategy.**"
>
> "People fall into the trap of thinking that **the plan for how they will build something new is
> itself a product strategy. This is especially common in early-stage companies or on teams
> lacking in strong product leadership.**"

> **The diagnostic:** if the document answers *what we will build and when*, it is a plan. If it
> answers *why this, for whom, and why us*, it is closer to a strategy. A plan that cannot
> explain why the alternatives were rejected is a list.

## Components

[S090] organises product strategy into three groups. This is one vendor's structure, but the
grouping is coherent and maps onto other material in this knowledge base.

| **Business models** | "Useful when you launch a new product or want to evolve your strategy as the market changes." Named types: "lean canvas, Porter's 5 forces, and the 10Ps marketing matrix" | *(not covered in the corpus — gap)* |
| **Positioning** | "Define where your product fits in the marketplace and the unique benefits you provide" | [positioning.md](positioning.md) |

|---|---|---|

| **Competitive analysis** | "Understand where your offering excels and falls short compared to other solutions... provides insights into where you fit within the broader market" | [../03-market-intelligence/competitive-analysis.md](../03-market-intelligence/competitive-analysis.md) |

### Imperatives — what you will accomplish
| Component | [S090] | Covered in |
|---|---|---|
| **Goals** | "Benchmarks that you want to achieve in the next quarter, year, or 18 months." Suggested exercise: "plot out potentials on a chart based on the **investment needed** (from your team) and the **impact** (to your customers)" | [product-goals.md](product-goals.md) |
| **Initiatives** | "High-level efforts or big themes of work that need to be implemented to achieve your goals. These strategic initiatives **link your goals to everything planned on your product roadmap**" | [../05-planning/roadmaps.md](../05-planning/roadmaps.md) |

### A constraint on imperatives
[S090] adds a warning that is easy to skip:

> "It can be tempting to choose many goals and many initiatives. You want to make everyone happy,
> right? **A product that serves everyone actually serves no one. Too many strategic imperatives
> will confuse the team and lead to muddy decision-making.** So you want to be shrewd when
> selecting goals and initiatives — **fewer, specific objectives** will make it easier to select
> features that will make a real impact."

## Why strategy matters

[S090] argues from risk:

> "Product development is an adventure. And like most adventures, it is full of risk. **Timing and
> ambiguity are two of the greatest threats** — neither of which can be fully controlled...
> **Lack of depth in understanding customer needs, the competitive landscape, and technical
> feasibility** can stunt progress and in some cases lead to failure."

What a strategy enables [S090]: align around goals; chart the way forward; prioritize the right
work; stay focused and agile; deliver what users need; create value for customers and the
business.

Its most decision-relevant claim: "**It enables folks to make better decisions faster and
prioritize features that will deliver the most value.**"

[S090] also offers a competitive framing: "**Strategy is an adaptation to achieve success. It
explains why a company will compete in a certain market and how what it delivers will be better
than any other company in that market at serving customers.**"

## Types of product strategy

[S090] presents nine, with the important caveat that these are **generic postures**, not
company-specific insights: "the individual companies are not doing anything unique. Instead they
are employing a proven product strategy in an effective way."

Its core categories: "**cost, quality, technology, service, and speed to market.** You may choose
one or several for your own product, **but typically not all.**"

| Strategy | Definition [S090] |
|---|---|
| **Challenger** | "Positioning your product as more exciting than others available in the market based on features, design, or efficiency" |
| **Cost** | "Positioning your product as the most affordable among competitors to build your customer base" |
| **Intellectual property** | "Highlighting proprietary technology that sets your product apart" |
| **Focus** | "Developing products for a specific and well-defined customer segment" |
| **Niche** | "Focusing on the needs of a specific (even narrow) group of users" |
| **Quality** | "Delivering the highest quality product in terms of design, functionality, data, and reliability" |
| **Service** | "Providing exceptional service and support to customers as an extension of your offering" |
| **Speed** | "Prioritizing production efficiency so you can shorten the time from concept to delivery" |
| **Upselling** | "Looking at ways to solve adjacent problems for existing customers in an effort to encourage upgrades or new purchases" |

### Why there is no "differentiation strategy"
[S090] makes a point worth preserving:

> "You might be wondering if we missed the 'differentiation strategy.' Keen eye, but no — this is
> an **intentional omission.** The strategies above are all ways that you show why your product is
> different and worthy of a customer's purchase. **You cannot have a differentiation strategy,
> when the goal of any product strategy is to differentiate in a meaningful way.**"

> This is a genuinely useful correction: "we will differentiate" is not a strategic choice, it is
> a restatement of the objective. The choice is *on what axis*.

## Strategy is ongoing

[S090]: "Product strategy is ongoing. **Budgets, tools, and the market will change. Each shift
can prompt changes in your overall strategy** — adjusting plans, removing fruitless efforts, and
exploring new opportunities."

## On using AI to set strategy

[S090] includes a position worth recording because it is directly relevant to a PM agent's own
role:

> "Prompt AI to **play devil's advocate.** If the output reveals anything worth considering, go
> back and patch any weak areas of your product strategy... This strengthens every iteration of
> your strategy until you land on something the entire team can sign off on.
>
> **Know that AI is not meant to create the strategy outright, though. Only you as a product
> manager have the judgment and deep understanding to do that well.**"

> **Status: vendor opinion, undated within an Aug 2026 update, no evidence.** It is recorded
> because it is one of the very few statements in the corpus about AI's role in product
> decisions, and because the *adversarial* use it proposes — stress-testing rather than
> generating — is a defensible division of labour. It should not be treated as a settled position.
> See [RESEARCH_BACKLOG.md](../RESEARCH_BACKLOG.md).

## Limitations

- **[S090] is a product-management software vendor**, and the article is heavily interleaved with
  its own templates and tooling arguments. Those have been excluded; the conceptual content is
  retained.
- **The strategy *types* table is a taxonomy of generic competitive postures**, closer to
  classical business strategy than to product strategy specifically. The corpus contains no
  primary strategy literature (Porter, Rumelt, etc.) to check it against — [S090] links to
  *HBR: What Is Strategy, Again?* but that source is not in the corpus.
- **No treatment of strategy *formulation*** — how to actually arrive at a strategic choice, as
  opposed to what a strategy document should contain. [S106] in
  [strategic-thinking.md](strategic-thinking.md) is the closest the corpus comes, and it operates
  at the level of individual decisions rather than strategy.
- **No coverage of business models** despite [S090] naming them as a foundation component.
- **No coverage of strategy at portfolio or company level**, or of how product strategies
  reconcile across multiple products.

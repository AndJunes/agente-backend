# Strategic Thinking and Product Decisions

## The reframe

[S106] is **Ravi Mehta** — former CPO at Tinder, former VP Product at Tripadvisor. It is the
corpus's most substantive treatment of how product decisions are actually made, and it begins by
demystifying the subject:

> "I had in my mind that strategy was some big, amorphous topic that requires years of experience,
> lots of frameworks, and a rare, natural gift... **The people who are strong strategic thinkers
> aren't necessarily pulling from some encyclopedic playbook. They haven't memorized Sun Tzu's
> *The Art of War* or spent years at McKinsey.**
>
> Instead, **the best strategic thinkers understand the future outcomes that they're working
> towards and make decisions that have the highest likelihood of achieving those outcomes.** In
> other words, the best strategic thinkers **predict the future based on what they know today,
> and make decisions based on that prediction.**"

## Decisions are the unit of strategy

> "The future unfolds based on the decisions that we make today. Good decisions create valuable
> outcomes in the future, and bad decisions lead to unnecessary risk. So, we can think of
> **decisions as the basic building block of strategy.**" [S106]

> "We can make those decisions **tactically** by thinking only about the immediate moment, or we
> can think about each decision **strategically — a step towards unlocking a valuable sequence of
> future options, outcomes, and opportunities.**" [S106]

Its examples of decisions at different altitudes: "some short-term like *'What should this button
say?'* and others that can have impact for months or years to come such as *'What should our OKRs
be?'*, *'What should we prioritize for the roadmap?'*, and *'What principles underpin our
product?'*"

## What makes a decision "good"

This distinction is the document's most transferable idea:

> "**A good decision is not necessarily a 'right' decision** — that is, a decision that has a
> successful outcome. We're predicting the future, and we can't ever know for certain whether
> we'll be right. Every decision involves risk — and sometimes more risk means more opportunity
> for upside. So **a good decision is one that strikes the right balance of risk and reward.**"
> [S106]

> **Consequence for how decisions should be reviewed:** judging a decision by its outcome
> ("resulting") will systematically punish sound decisions that didn't pay off and reward reckless
> ones that did. This is not stated by [S106] but follows directly from its definition.

## The process: a four-step decision analysis

[S106] calls this the *Decision Making Time Machine*:

1. **Identify the challenge you are facing and the decision you need to make.**
2. **Analyze what success looks like** using the decision factors below.
3. **Use those same factors to analyze the failure case.**
4. **Make the decision** — "you'll likely be surprised at how easy it is to make a confident
   decision now that you've envisioned how it might play out. This final decision could be to
   **move forward, revise your approach, or forego the decision entirely.**"

### Step 1 is the hard one
[S106]: "A good decision starts by being clear and specific about the challenge at hand, and the
decision that must be made to overcome that challenge. **But this first step is often the hardest.
Indecision is like quicksand and can easily lead to feeling stuck or overwhelmed.**"

### Define exactly two outcomes
> "Instead of imagining all the things that might happen, **define just two outcomes: success and
> failure.** A simple outcome tree helps us think about the future in concrete terms." [S106]

Its example: a founder's definition of success varies — "A freelancer or bootstrapper may define
success as replacing their salary, another founder may be focused on a venture-scale exit." And
failure too must be defined: "Importantly, **failure isn't all negative** — founders gain valuable
experience even if their venture doesn't succeed."

### The critical rule: analyze them independently

> "**The most common flaw in strategic thinking is to assume that success and failure are equal
> and opposite. Our brains are lazy so we like to infer one answer from another.**
>
> For example, we may inadvertently assume that the chances of success and failure are roughly
> 50/50. **This is rarely the case** and gets exacerbated by the fact that people have a very hard
> time estimating probabilities.
>
> We may also think that risks and rewards are roughly equal. **Again, this is rarely the case and
> fraught with cognitive biases.**" [S106]

And later: "A low probability of failure may not mean a high probability of success. A significant
consequence of failure may not be counterbalanced by significant rewards. **Not fair, but that's
life. Good strategic thinking embraces that reality.**"

## The six decision factors

Assess each **separately for success and for failure.**

### 1. Probability — "What is the likelihood of succeeding or failing?"
### 2. Consequence — "What is the impact of succeeding or failing?"

[S106] treats these together because "**our lizard brain often conflates the two.**" Its
illustration:

> "Consider car crashes versus shark attacks. Most people are more scared of shark attacks than
> car crashes — but the risk of driving to the beach is far greater than swimming in the ocean.
> There are around **70 shark attacks a year worldwide, and about 6 million car accidents per year
> in the US** alone. Shark attacks are very unlikely, but the consequences of being attacked by a
> shark are always severe. **Our fear center has a hard time separating the likelihood of risk from
> the consequence of risk.**"

*(The two figures are cited without source and should be treated as illustrative, not as data.)*

**The 2×2** — likelihood of failure against consequence of failure:

| | Low consequence | High consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **High likelihood** | **Tolerable** — "A kid learning to ride a bike... they can always get up, dust themselves off and go back to it" | **Reckless** — "a person climbing Mt. Everest without proper training" |
| **Low likelihood** | **Safe** — "the easiest decisions to make" | **Calculated** — "With proper training... the consequence of failure is still high, but training can help a person lower the probability of failure. In this case, **training helps a person move the decision from reckless to calculated**" |

> **The actionable insight:** you usually cannot change the *consequence* of failure, but you can
> often change its *probability*. Moving a decision from reckless to calculated is what
> preparation, research and de-risking actually buy. This is the same logic as validating the
> riskiest assumption first — see
> [../02-discovery/hypotheses-and-assumptions.md](../02-discovery/hypotheses-and-assumptions.md).

[S106] notes the same 2×2 works for the success side: "looking at the probability of success vs.
the rewards from that success."

### 3. Latency — "How long will it take to succeed or fail?"

> "**People are really bad at dealing with latency between action and outcome.** The classic
> example is trying to adjust the shower temperature if it's delayed — people get frustrated and
> **overcompensate** leading to a whipsaw between water that is way too hot and way too cold. In
> other cases, people **underreact** — not realizing that a decision is going off course before
> it's too late." [S106]

The response: "**identifying these latencies and putting in place systems to proactively monitor
how a decision is progressing.**"

Latency is also asymmetric between cases: "success can play out very quickly (such as a successful
product launch) but it may take time to recognize that a launch has failed. In other scenarios,
such as the outcome from a surgery, the opposite is true — **failure is immediately apparent but
it can take months or years to evaluate success.**"

### 4. Cost — "What will it cost to achieve success or failure?"

> "In particular, **we want to minimize the cost to reach failure so that we can quickly and
> inexpensively learn what isn't working. This is the first principle underlying the concept of
> the MVP** or 'minimum viable product'. **A good MVP is the least costly way to prove out an
> idea — the least costly way to achieve success or recognize failure.**" [S106]

> This is the corpus's clearest statement of *why* an MVP exists, and it is more useful than the
> definitional treatment elsewhere. Note the framing: the MVP optimises **cost-to-learn**, not
> scope. See [../02-discovery/discovery-frameworks.md](../02-discovery/discovery-frameworks.md)
> and the MVP gap recorded there.

### 5. Reversibility — "Can we 'redo' the decision in the future?"

[S106], attributing to **Jeff Bezos**: "**one-way doors and two-way doors.** Some decisions can be
reversed, and others cannot... Jeff goes on to say that **most decisions are two-way doors. In
these cases, we should have a bias towards action.** We can make these types of decisions quickly
without worrying about the downside."

"But this is not always the case. There are some decisions that are one-way. **We need care and
conviction before making these types of decisions — because there is no going back.**"

**Worked product example** [S106]:
- **Two-way door:** "the decision on whether to launch a product at the end of the quarter... If
  we ship the wrong product and people are frustrated, that's OK. **We can iterate on it.**"
- **One-way door:** "the decision to do a **Product Hunt launch** for that product. If we launch
  and the product isn't ready, then **we've used up our one shot.**"

> **The same underlying product may present both kinds of decision.** The distinction is not about
> the thing being decided but about whether the attempt is repeatable.

[S031] independently names **reversibility** as a prioritization criterion — "ease of rollback or
containment if adoption or quality disappoints." See
[../05-planning/prioritization-frameworks.md](../05-planning/prioritization-frameworks.md).

### 6. Second-order effects — "What is now possible as a result of success or failure?"

> "People often only think about the immediate outcome of their decision. **In chess, the #1
> factor in a player's ability to win is how many moves ahead they are able to think** — in other
> words, their ability to understand second and third-order consequences." [S106]

Two second-order effects [S106] singles out:

| Effect | Description |
|---|---|
| **Optionality** | "Many decisions can increase or reduce optionality... **What options have opened up and what options have closed?** For example, **the decision to lower prices may open up the option to sell to a different customer profile, but may also take high-cost customer acquisition channels off the table.**" |
| **Feedback loops** | "Some decisions have one-time results so it's pretty easy to weigh the return on investment. **Other decisions lead to outcomes that compound over time. People often underestimate the long-term return on decisions that create feedback loops.** This is exacerbated by the fact that feedback loops often have some initial latency." |

## A worked example — including its outcome

[S106] applies the framework to the author's own 2020 decision to start a VC-backed company
(Outpace). The analysis is preserved here because its **honesty about the result** is unusual.

- **Defining success personally rather than by convention:** "The goal of venture firms is to
  return their entire fund with a single investment... So, success often begins at the $1B
  valuation mark. **I believe entrepreneurs need to define their personal success differently.**"
  His own definition: "a $30M-$100M acquisition by a company I wanted to be a part of."
- **Analysing failure produced the key insight:** "In the world of startups, it's possible to
  **fail fast or fail slow**... many founders I spoke with **don't regret failing — they just wish
  they would have failed faster.**" Estimating 12–18 months to call product/market fit and another
  12–18 to scale growth, "failing fast likely requires **2-3 years.**"
- **Probabilities that don't sum to 100%:** "maybe a 30% chance of this happening — so **10%
  probability that we succeed with a substantive exit, 30% that we wind down the company in 2-4
  years, and 60% of something outside of our success and failure cases.** Remember, **the success
  and failure cases don't need to add up to 100% — and probably shouldn't.**"
- **The reversibility insight, inverted:** "starting a company was a **two-way door** decision. I
  could always go back to a product executive role. But, importantly, **NOT starting a company was
  a one-way door decision.** I was never going to have a better opportunity to take the leap than
  that moment."
- **The outcome:** "As I write this, **we've made the difficult decision to wind Outpace down
  after running out of runway.**" And: "Not only did this framework help me get the conviction I
  needed to take the risk... **it also gave me the perspective I needed to be at peace with the
  outcome.**"

> The venture failed and the author still endorses the decision. That is the definition of a good
> decision from the top of this document, demonstrated rather than asserted.

## Why the classical strategy vocabulary is not required

[S106] closes with a claim worth recording:

> "We made it through a whole post about strategic thinking **without mentioning competitive
> advantage, economies of scale, market power, network effects, game theory**, or the myriad other
> concepts packed into stacks of strategy books.
>
> These concepts are **powerful shorthands**... But they can all be refined down to the first
> principles we just reviewed. **Competitive advantage** helps us understand the probability and
> consequence of success. **Economies of scale** is a way of thinking about latency and cost.
> **Network effects and game theory** are tools for understanding second-order effects."

> This is a strong reductionist claim and is not defended in detail. Treat it as a useful framing
> for *approaching* strategy without formal training, not as a demonstration that the classical
> concepts are redundant.

## Weaker material on the same topic

[S107] and [S006] both address "how to think more strategically" at a much lower level of
substance.

[S107] (Mind the Product) collects practitioner quotes; its usable content is the observation that
strategic thinking involves seeing where things "unexpectedly intersect," attributed to Miles
Skorpen. It names further reading (*Leadership in a Digital World* by James Gadsby Peet;
*Strategy Needs Good Words* by Martin Weigel) that is **not in the corpus**.

[S006] (Institute of Product Leadership) offers four generic suggestions: ask questions; observe
and reflect; consider opposing ideas; get formal training. Its one substantive point:

> "Once you've landed on your strategy... **it's good to question your assumptions and put your
> hypothesis through rigorous testing. This helps you to ensure that you are not just overlooking
> other possibilities. It's about playing devil's advocate with your ideas** and that can really
> help you identify blind spots or weaknesses and equip you to defend your strategy when others
> ask questions." [S006]

Its fourth suggestion — "take up formal training" — resolves into a promotion for the publisher's
own programs and is excluded. [S106] explicitly contradicts the premise: strategic thinking is
"**not the domain of a rare few or something that requires years of study.**"

## Limitations

- **[S106] is one practitioner's framework**, presented without external evidence. Its
  illustrative statistics (shark attacks, car accidents, chess) are uncited.
- **The Bezos one-way/two-way door attribution is second-hand**; the primary source (Amazon
  shareholder letters) is not in the corpus.
- **No treatment of group decision-making** — how this framework works when the decision is made
  by a committee, or when stakeholders disagree on the definition of success.
- **No treatment of decision documentation** or how to record a decision so it can be reviewed
  later.
- The corpus contains **no formal strategy literature** to test [S106]'s reductionist claim
  against.

# Risk Response Strategies

## The complete set

[S065] (a PMI Authorized Training Partner) gives the full PMI list — **five strategies for negative
risk and five for positive risk, with *Escalate* and *Accept* appearing in both.**

| Negative (threats) | Positive (opportunities) |
|---|---|
| **Escalate** | **Escalate** |
| **Avoid** | **Exploit** |
| **Transfer** | **Share** |
| **Mitigate** | **Enhance** |
| **Accept** | **Accept** |

[S065]: "A project manager may use **any combination** of risk control techniques depending on the
circumstances of the project risk."

> **Note the symmetry.** Each negative strategy has a positive mirror: *avoid* (prevent it
> happening) ↔ *exploit* (ensure it happens); *transfer* (move the downside to a third party) ↔
> *share* (give a third party ownership of the upside); *mitigate* (reduce probability or impact) ↔
> *enhance* (increase probability or impact). Recognising the mirror makes the positive strategies
> intuitive rather than exotic.

## Negative risk responses

### Escalate
[S065]: "used when **a risk response authorization is needed from outside a project's team.**"

Example given: "if a customized shipping container cracks after the project closes, the risk will
be high for the next project requiring it. **Escalation, such as notifying the shipping manager of
the container damage, can help ensure a risk response is activated to help future projects.**"

> Escalation is not "telling someone about a problem." It is **reassigning ownership** to a level
> that can actually act on it — typically because the risk exceeds the project's authority or
> outlives the project.

### Avoid
**PMI definition, via [S065]:** "**eliminating the threat or protecting the project from its
impact.**"

[S065]: "Avoidance **eliminates the risk altogether when there is no risk tolerance.**"

Example: "if the project's computers have no internet access, you avoid malicious external software
attacks and the risk of losing data."

> Avoidance usually costs something else — the example trades away internet access. **Avoidance is
> a scope or approach change, not a control.**

### Transfer
**PMI definition, via [S065]:** "**shifting the impact of a threat to a third party.**"

[S065] calls it "**the most common risk response**" and notes it is "a deflection."

Example: "When a company outsources customer service operations... the risk of personnel
recruitment expenses will transfer from the project company to the vendor."

**The critical caveat** [S065]: "**Should the vendor fail to meet the requirements, the risk
transfers back to the project company to address.**"

> Transfer moves *financial consequence*, not *accountability*. Insurance and outsourcing do not
> make the outcome someone else's problem — they make the cost someone else's problem, and only
> while the counterparty performs.

### Mitigate
**PMI definition, via [S065]:** "**decreasing the probability of occurrence or impact of a
threat.**"

[S065]: "Mitigating risks means the risk is **just slightly above your organization's risk appetite
or tolerance level**, so you take steps to reduce the risk's impact to within acceptable limits.
**As it is decreased, not removed, there can be residual risk.**"

Example: "to mitigate theft, a company installs exterior security cameras. **The residual risk is
that a fire might destroy the building and its contents without internal warning systems.**"

> **Residual risk is the concept most often lost.** After mitigation you still hold risk, and it
> should be recorded and reassessed rather than treated as closed.

### Accept
**PMI definition, via [S065]:** "**not taking any action unless the risk occurs.**"

[S065]: "**The company's tolerance level for risk influences the use of the accept risk response.**"

Example continuing the security case: "the lack of a sprinkler system shows that they **accept the
risk of fire but do not accept the risk of theft.**"

> Acceptance is a *decision*, not an omission. The difference between accepting a risk and ignoring
> one is that the accepted risk is **identified, assessed, recorded, and consciously left
> untreated** — which means it can be revisited when tolerance or circumstances change.

## Positive risk responses

[S065]: "Positive risk response strategies are focused on **leveraging opportunities** for your
project. The risk can benefit the project, and **the risk response should maximize that.**"

### Escalate
"Used when a risk needs to be addressed by an authority beyond the project team." Example: "if a
potential customer asks for a one-time discount, the positive risk of gaining the business may be
escalated to the company owner to decide if the sale is worth it."

### Exploit
**PMI definition, via [S065]:** "**ensuring that an opportunity occurs.**"

[S065]: "the project manager may **delay, avoid, or activate specific project activities to increase
the probability of a risk occurring.**"

Example: "to exploit the positive risk of early delivery of a project deliverable, an incentive is
offered to the team."

### Share
**PMI definition, via [S065]:** allocating "**the ownership of an opportunity to a third party who
is best able to capture the benefit for the project.**"

Example: "If a manufacturer provides a part to help you meet new customer requirements, you may
share the risk of internal costs so that **you both benefit from increased sales.**"

### Enhance
**PMI definition, via [S065]:** "**increase the probability of occurrence or impact.**"

[S065]: "The opposite of eliminating risk, enhancement increases the possibility of occurrence."

Example: "If a vendor knows that certification will increase their preferred status, they may obtain
it to **enhance the opportunity of being selected for more government contracts.**"

### Accept
"Applies to negative and positive risks... when no action is taken." Example given: a company may
accept the risk of not winning certain contracts rather than incur the safety-protocol costs of
qualifying for them.

## Avoid versus mitigate

[S065] devotes a section to this distinction because it is the most commonly confused pair. The
worked example — managing the risk of being late to a PMP exam — did not fully extract, but the
principle is stated clearly elsewhere in the source:

| | Avoid | Mitigate |
|---|---|---|
| **Effect on the risk** | "Eliminating the threat" | "Decreasing the probability of occurrence or impact" |
| **Residual risk** | None — the risk is removed | **Yes** — "as it is decreased, not removed, there can be residual risk" |
| **When appropriate** | "When there is **no risk tolerance**" | When the risk is "**just slightly above** your organization's risk appetite" |
| **Typical cost** | A change of approach or scope | An added control |

> **The decision rule implied:** consult risk tolerance first. A risk far above tolerance calls for
> avoidance or transfer; one marginally above calls for mitigation; one within tolerance calls for
> acceptance.

## An alternative vocabulary

[S012] (Mitti/SafetyCulture) presents a partly overlapping list from a general business-risk
perspective rather than a PMI one. Where the terms differ, **the concepts are close but not
identical** — noted because both vocabularies appear in practice.

| [S012] term | Definition | "Best used for" [S012] |
|---|---|---|
| **Risk assessment** | *(treated by [S012] as a strategy rather than an analysis step)* | "Identifying risks across all areas of the business" |
| **Risk avoidance** | "Avoiding activities that may lead to potential harm, loss, or adverse results... **eliminating any involvement or exposure**" | "Avoiding situations with **high stakes and significant negative consequences**" |
| **Risk transference** | "Transferring or shifting **the financial consequences** of a particular identified risk to another party" — "Your company can become a policyholder that transfers risks to an insurance company... in exchange for a premium" | "Handling **high-stake risks that are unlikely to occur**" |
| **Risk reduction** | "Designing **processes, controls, and measures** that aim to reduce the risks... Each business typically develops a unique set of internal controls" | "Solving **operational and safety-related** risks" |
| **Risk retention** | "**Accepting and internally managing** risks rather than relying on external parties like insurance, joint ventures, and outsourcing... companies may add supplementary resources — time, workforce, and budget — to mitigate the impact or cover [losses]" | "Managing **low-impact or low-probability** risks" |
| **Employee training and development** | "Address the challenges of **human error** in the workplace by providing regular employee training" | — |

### Mapping between the two vocabularies

| PMI [S065] | Approximate [S012] equivalent | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Avoid | Risk avoidance | Essentially the same |
| Transfer | Risk transference | [S012] is explicit that it is the **financial** consequence being moved |
| Mitigate | Risk reduction | [S012] emphasises **controls and process design** |
| Accept | Risk retention | [S012] adds that retention may involve **reserving resources** to absorb the impact — closer to PMI's contingency reserve than to passive acceptance |
| Escalate | — | **No equivalent in [S012]** |
| Exploit / Share / Enhance | — | **No positive-risk strategies in [S012] at all** |

> **[S012]'s "best used for" column is its main contribution**, because it maps strategies onto the
> probability/impact quadrants: transfer for high-impact/low-probability, retention for
> low-impact/low-probability, reduction for recurring operational risk. That mapping is absent from
> the PMI-derived source.

## Limitations

- **Both sources are commercial:** [S065] is PMP exam preparation; [S012] is a GRC software
  vendor's topic guide whose examples are safety- and insurance-oriented rather than software-
  product-oriented.
- **PMBOK is not in the corpus**; all PMI definitions are quoted second-hand.
- **The avoid-vs-mitigate worked example did not extract** from [S065].
- **No treatment of contingency reserves, fallback plans, or triggers** for executing a response —
  although [S043] names risk triggers in the monitoring context. See
  [risk-monitoring.md](risk-monitoring.md).
- **No treatment of secondary risks** — risks introduced *by* a response.
- **No software-specific examples.** Every example here is physical, financial or administrative.
  See [product-risk.md](product-risk.md) for the product-side treatment.

# Mind Mapping

## Definition

[S135] (IxDF): "Mind maps are **visual diagrams that structure information around a central idea,
with linked ideas branching out.** Designers use them to **organize thoughts, analyze information
and develop creative solutions.** Mind maps give a clear structure and hierarchy of thoughts and
make complex concepts easier to understand. **They include a central idea, topics and subtopics
showing as nodes, and branches connecting these nodes.**"

[S023] (Aha!) notes an alternative name: "a mind map (also referred to as a '**concept map**')."

## How to build one

[S136]:
1. "Place your **central topic or problem** in the middle of your workspace"
2. "Branch out with **major themes or categories** related to the central idea"
3. "Add sub-branches with **specific ideas, examples, or questions**"
4. "**Connect related concepts across different branches to surface insights**"

[S061] (Mural) gives a five-step variant that adds a convergence step:
1. "Identify the primary topic"
2. "Branch out into subtopics"
3. "Connect related topics"
4. "**Select the top themes and categories**"
5. "Delve deeper into niched-down ideas"

> Step 4 in [S061]'s version is the important addition: a mind map that only expands produces a
> diagram, not a decision. Compare the screening step in
> [brainwriting.md](brainwriting.md).

## Why it works

[S136]: "Mind maps match how teams actually think — **associatively rather than linearly.** They
help **remote teams see the same mental model simultaneously**, preventing the '**wait, are we
talking about the same thing?**' confusion that wastes meeting time."

[S023]: "Mind mapping is a great way for your product team to **take a holistic look at problems to
solve — starting with broad angles and getting to more specific ideas with each branch.**"

> The claim worth keeping is the **shared-mental-model** one. A mind map's primary product may be
> alignment rather than ideas — the same secondary benefit [S136] attributes to brainstorming
> generally.

## When to use it

[S136]: "**Early-stage problem exploration** when you need to understand **how different aspects of
a challenge relate to each other.**"

> Note that this is a *problem-space* technique, not a solution-generation one. It maps structure;
> it does not evaluate.

## Variants

[S060] (Miro) names several diagram types under the mind-mapping umbrella. The corpus's
descriptions are thin, so they are recorded as a vocabulary rather than as method:

| Type | As listed in [S060] |
|---|---|
| **Tree map** | Hierarchical; related forms named include org-chart variants ("Dotted Line Org Chart", "Inverted Org Chart") |
| **Bubble map** | With a "**Double Bubble Map**" variant — conventionally used for comparison |
| **Brace map** | Whole-to-parts decomposition |
| **Circle map** | Related forms named include "The Golden Circles" |

> [S060] names these without explaining when each applies. Their inclusion here is for recognition
> only. **Do not infer the use case of a diagram type from its name alone.**

## Uses across the corpus

Mind maps appear in several other contexts, which indicates they are a general-purpose structuring
tool rather than an ideation-only technique:

| Use | Source |
|---|---|

## Limitations

- **All sources are collaboration-software vendors** (Miro, Mural, Aha!, IxDF). Descriptions are
  brief and interleaved with tooling.
- **No evidence** of effectiveness relative to other structuring methods.
- **[S135] and the IxDF entries are one-page glossary stubs.**
- **The variant diagram types in [S060] are named but not explained** (see above).
- **No guidance on when a mind map stops being useful** — e.g. at what size it becomes unreadable,
  or how to convert one into a decision or a backlog.
- **Tony Buzan**, the figure most associated with popularising mind mapping, **is not mentioned
  anywhere in the corpus**, nor is any origin account given. Recorded as a gap.

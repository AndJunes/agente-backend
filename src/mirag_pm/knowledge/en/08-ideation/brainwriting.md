# Brainwriting and the 6-3-5 Method

> **Note on source quality.** [S010] (Wikipedia's *6-3-5 Brainwriting* article) is, by citation
> standards, **the best-sourced document in the entire corpus.** It cites Rohrbach's original 1969
> publication, a peer-reviewed engineering-design journal (Shah, *Journal of Mechanical Design*,
> 2000), and a marketing journal (VanGundy, *Journal of Consumer Marketing*, 1984). Wikipedia is
> not itself an authority, but it is the only source here that provides a traceable path to primary
> literature.

## Definition

[S129] (Chauncey Wilson, Smashing Magazine): "Rather than ask participants to yell out ideas (**a
serial process**), you ask them to **write down their ideas** about a particular question or
problem on sheets of paper for a few minutes; then, you have each participant **pass their ideas on
to someone else, who reads the ideas and adds new ideas.** After a few minutes, you ask the
participants to pass their papers to others, and the process repeats."

[S137] (IxDF): "Brainwriting is an ideation technique in which **participants write their ideas in
silence instead of speaking aloud.** Participants then share their ideas and build upon them.
**Brainwriting levels the field and encourages introverts and junior team members to participate
more equally.**"

## The problem it solves

[S129] states the case against conventional brainstorming directly:

> "Brainstorming is often the method of choice for ideation, but **it is fraught with problems that
> range from participants' fear of evaluation to the serial nature of the process — only one idea
> at a time.**"

Its account of why brainwriting outperforms it:

> "The number of ideas generated from brainwriting **often exceeds** what you'd expect from
> face-to-face brainstorming because you've **(1) reduced anxiety somewhat, (2) followed a parallel
> process in which a dozen people may add items simultaneously, and (3) reduced the amount of
> extraneous talk** that happens during brainstorming, which takes time away from idea generation."

> The second mechanism — **parallelism** — is the structural one. In verbal brainstorming only one
> person speaks at a time, so group size does not increase throughput. In brainwriting it does.
> *(This is the phenomenon known in the research literature as production blocking; the corpus does
> not name it, but VanGundy 1984, cited by [S010], is a source on exactly this comparison.)*

[S010] adds a different rationale: the technique "is meant to **overcome the possible creativity
barriers brought up by issues such as interpersonal conflicts, different cultural backgrounds, and
reasons of intellectual properties.**"

## The 6-3-5 method

### Origin
[S010]: "developed by **Bernd Rohrbach** who originally published it in a German sales magazine,
**Absatzwirtschaft, in 1968**." *(The article's own reference list dates the cited publication to
**1969**: Rohrbach, Bernd (1969). "Kreativ nach Regeln – Methode 635, eine neue Technik zum Lösen
von Problemen". Absatzwirtschaft. 12: 73–75. **The 1968/1969 discrepancy is in the source;
it is not resolvable from the corpus.**)*

### The name and the arithmetic
[S010]: "**6 participants** supervised by a moderator who are required to write down **3 ideas** on
a specific worksheet within **5 minutes**; this is also the etymology of the methodology's name.
The outcome after 6 rounds... is **108 ideas generated in 30 minutes.**"

### Scaling
[S010] gives exact yields for other group sizes:

| Participants | Ideas generated |
|---|---|
| 4 | 48 |
| 5 | 75 |
| **6** | **108** |
| 7 | 147 |

"The optimum application would require 6 participants, as **too many would make the session
unmanageable.**"

### Procedure
[S010]:

1. **Ensure shared background knowledge.** "It is fundamental to assure that all participants share
   a deep background knowledge on the topic, since **even a single not-well-informed individual can
   significantly affect the quality of the output.**"
2. **Define the problem first.** "Through a preliminary discussion, the group focuses on
   **identifying the problem to be solved or the aim to be pursued.**"
3. **Write the problem statement on the Idea Form.** The worksheet "consists of a grid where the
   heading of the columns are Idea 1, Idea 2 and Idea 3 and **the rows identify the name of who has
   contributed** to that particular suggestion."
4. **Five minutes, in silence, for the first row.** Ideas "may be expressed in **any graphical
   form: written, drawn, through a symbol**, or however the author prefers."
5. **Pass to the right on the moderator's signal.**
6. **Repeat.** "Each participant is free to **get inspired from the idea he reads on the sheet
   written by his neighbour and contribute to them by integrating or completing them, or decide to
   ignore them and start a new one from scratch.**"
7. **Continue until the worksheet is full.** "If the supervisor deems it necessary, the time for
   each round may be **extended to a maximum of 10 minutes.**"
8. **Screen and select.** "A preliminary screening... where **exact duplicates are deleted**, and a
   team evaluation takes place, perhaps using the **Nominal Group Technique or Prioritization
   matrix**, to select **1 to 3 ideas** the group can focus on."

> Step 8 is easy to skip and essential: 108 ideas are not an output, they are raw material.
> Convergence is a separate activity with its own method. See
> [idea-evaluation.md](idea-evaluation.md).

### Advantages
[S010]:
- "A very **straightforward method**, therefore easy and quick to learn. **No particular training
  for the supervisor is required.**"
- "**Valorises the possible different backgrounds of participants**, since it encourages sharing and
  exchanging knowledge."
- "In contrast to traditional brainstorming, it **assures active participation from all members**,
  and at the same time **avoids issues of domination over introverts**, who are also likely to feel
  more free about expressing their own ideas **instead of potentially having their potential
  inhibited by those who shout louder.**"
- "**All ideas are recorded on the worksheet**; this means that nobody has to be in charge of
  taking notes... and it adds a motivational factor, since it is **possible to keep track of the
  author** of a particular idea."

### Disadvantages
[S010] — and these are real constraints, not formalities:
- "Expressing ideas in written form may lead to **issues in clarity**, due to participants having
  trouble summarising their ideas or **reading their colleagues' handwriting** or graphical
  representations."
- "The system's constraints might require **additional time for some people to become familiar**
  with the methodology."
- "There is a **risk of clash of similar ideas, since there is no immediate group discussion**,
  which constitutes a loss of possible innovation."

> The third is the significant trade-off: **brainwriting buys parallelism and psychological safety
> at the cost of real-time synthesis.** Verbal brainstorming allows a half-formed idea to be
> completed by someone else in the moment; brainwriting defers that to the review stage.

## Variants

[S129] names four approaches: "**interactive brainwriting, the 6-3-5 method, the idea card method,
and the remote spreadsheet method.**"

> Only 6-3-5 and interactive brainwriting are described in the corpus; the idea card and remote
> spreadsheet methods are named only. **The remote spreadsheet method is the most relevant
> omission** for distributed teams. Recorded in [RESEARCH_BACKLOG.md](../RESEARCH_BACKLOG.md).

**Interactive brainwriting** [S129] follows the same pass-and-add pattern with a looser structure:
introduce the procedure, hand out paper, write, pass, repeat, and after 10–15 minutes "**collect the
sheets and post them for immediate discussion.**"

## When to use brainwriting

[S129]'s conditions are unusually specific and directly actionable:

| Condition | [S129] |
|---|---|
| **Group is too large for brainstorming** | "You could conduct brainwriting at a conference of **500 people** simply by leaving a large card on each seat, asking a question, and then having each audience member pass a card to someone else" |
| **Quiet participants** | "You have quiet people in your group who are **intimidated** by traditional brainstorming" |
| **Hierarchical or risk-averse culture** | "You are working in a culture in which brainstorming about 'wild ideas' or **expressing ideas that diverge from those of senior management is not accepted**" |
| **Time is limited** | "I've used brainwriting... when I had **only 10 minutes** to get feedback from the product team. I ended up with **more than 50 different questions**, without the fuss of having to set up a formal brainstorming session" |
| **No experienced moderator** | "**Brainstorming, contrary to what many blog posts claim, is difficult to do well.** Brainwriting, in contrast, requires that you be able to **ask a question, read a clock and collect answers**" |
| **Dominant personalities present** | "You are worried about **loud or forceful individuals influencing others**" |

### A distinctive use: surfacing differences between groups
[S129]:

> "Brainwriting can be used to **understand how different groups view an issue.** You might try to
> conduct **separate brainwriting sessions with different internal groups.** For example, if you
> asked groups to brainwrite about '**What are the most important problems faced by our
> customers?**' you might find that developers have a different perspective from the UX team, who
> have a different perspective from product managers. **In my experience, the differences emerge
> more strongly through brainwriting than through face-to-face brainstorming.**"

> This is a genuinely useful application for a PM: it turns an ideation technique into an
> **alignment diagnostic.** It also corroborates, by a different route, the measured PM/UX
> disagreement in
> [../01-foundations/pm-and-ux-collaboration.md](../01-foundations/pm-and-ux-collaboration.md).

## When to avoid brainwriting

[S129] — this section is why the document is trustworthy:

- "There may be times when your colleagues **find it difficult to express ideas in writing.**"
- "**If you are working on complex issues**, then you might want to opt for small group
  brainstorming or another ideation technique **that allows for clarification and discussion.**"
- "**If you are forming a team**, then you might want to opt for traditional brainstorming, because
  participants will be more familiar with that method... and **the social interaction will foster
  team-building.**"
- "You might want to avoid brainwriting **if you are in a culture with strict rules about which
  methods and procedures to follow.** People who follow a highly structured product design and
  development process might find brainwriting a bit too radical."

## Related use in risk work

[S125] and [S100] both list **brainstorming** and the **Nominal Group Technique** among risk
identification techniques. [S100]'s framing — "verbal, partly written, or **nominal group**
brainstorm" — indicates that written and nominal-group variants are standard practice there too.

Brainwriting's anonymity property makes it a close relative of the **Delphi technique**, which
[S100] describes for risk identification: "consult a group of experts **anonymously**." See
[../10-risk/risk-identification.md](../10-risk/risk-identification.md).

## Limitations

- **The productivity claims are experiential, not measured, in the sources we have.** [S129] says
  brainwriting "often yields more ideas in less time" based on the author's teaching and consulting
  experience. [S010] cites literature that appears to address exactly this comparison
  (VanGundy 1984, "Brain Writing for New Product Ideas: An alternative to Brainstorming"; Shah 2000
  on "Evaluation of Idea Generation Methods") — **but those papers are not in the corpus and their
  findings are not reported.**
- **More ideas is not the same as better ideas.** No source in the corpus addresses idea *quality*
  under either method.
- **[S129] is dated December 2013** and its remote-collaboration guidance predates modern
  distributed-work tooling. The technique is paper-based in both descriptions.
- **[S137] is a one-page glossary stub** contributing only the definition.
- The 1968/1969 publication-date discrepancy is unresolved.

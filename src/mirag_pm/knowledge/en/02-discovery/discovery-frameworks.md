# Product Discovery Frameworks

## How to use this document

Six frameworks appear in the corpus. They are **not alternatives to each other in a single
decision** — they operate at different scopes. Roughly:

- **Double Diamond** and **Dual-track** describe *process shape*.
- **Design Sprint** is a *time-boxed intervention*.
- **Lean Startup** is a *validation loop*.
- **Opportunity Solution Tree** is a *structuring and prioritization artefact*.
- **JTBD** is a *lens for interpreting customer behaviour*.

Attribution and dates below are as reported by [S110]. Where the corpus contains no primary
source, that is flagged.

---

## 1. Dual-Track (Discovery and Delivery)

**What it is** [S110]: "a product development framework that separates the discovery and
delivery phases, enabling continuous discovery of user needs while simultaneously delivering
product features."

**Attributed origin** [S110]: "The concept of Dual-Track Agile owes its beginnings to a paper
by Desiree Sy, then at Autodesk, published in 2007."
> The corpus does not contain this paper. Attribution **UNVERIFIED** against a primary source.

**How to apply** [S110]:
1. Set up two parallel workstreams — one for discovery (validating ideas, gathering feedback),
   one for delivery (implementing and shipping).
2. **Discovery track:** user research, prototyping, testing hypotheses — "to continuously
   identify and refine the most valuable features to be developed."
3. **Delivery track:** take validated ideas and develop them into shippable features,
   following Agile practices such as sprints, daily stand-ups, continuous integration.
4. **Regular syncs between tracks** — "to avoid disconnects and ensure that the most valuable
   features are prioritized and built."
5. **Iterate and adjust** — "using insights from the delivery process to inform further
   discovery, and vice versa."

**When it fits** [S110]: dynamic environments where user needs and market conditions change
rapidly; organizations focused on iterative development such as startups or tech companies;
"when there is a need to balance exploring new ideas with the timely delivery of product
increments."

**Limitations / assumptions:**
- Requires enough capacity to run two tracks; a single-person product function cannot
  genuinely parallelise both.
- The named failure mode is **track disconnect** — [S110] builds an explicit sync step in
  precisely because the two tracks drift apart.
- The corpus gives no guidance on how far ahead discovery should run relative to delivery.

## 2. Design Sprint

**What it is** [S110]: "a five-day process for rapidly solving problems, designing solutions,
and validating ideas through prototyping and testing with real users."

**Attributed origin** [S110]: "developed by Jake Knapp at Google Ventures (GV) in 2010. It was
designed to condense months of work into a single week."
> Primary source (Knapp's book *Sprint*) is **not in the corpus**.

**The five days** [S110]:

| Day | Name | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Understand** | Define the problem and set a long-term goal; map the challenge; decide on a target to focus on |
| 2 | **Sketch** | Generate a wide range of ideas by sketching; each member **independently** creates detailed solutions |
| 3 | **Decide** | Review sketches, discuss pros and cons, choose the most promising; create a storyboard of the user journey |
| 4 | **Prototype** | Build a realistic prototype — "just good enough to test the concept with users, focusing on speed rather than perfection" |
| 5 | **Test** | Test with real users and gather feedback; the outcome "should guide the next steps, whether it's refining the idea, pivoting, or moving forward with development" |

**When it fits** [S110]: "a significant problem or challenge that requires innovative thinking
and rapid iteration"; validating a new idea or solving a complex problem "without committing
extensive resources"; situations "where there is uncertainty about the direction of a product
or feature."

**Limitations / assumptions:**
- Assumes a team can be freed for five consecutive days, and that the decision-maker is in
  the room on day 3.
- Tests a prototype, not a product — evidence is about comprehension and desirability, not
  sustained behaviour or technical feasibility at scale.
- Day-2 independent sketching assumes the problem is framed well enough to sketch against;
  it is not a substitute for the problem-space research in a discovery.

## 3. Jobs To Be Done (JTBD)

Summarised here for comparison; treated in depth in
[jobs-to-be-done.md](jobs-to-be-done.md).

**What it is** [S110]: "a framework that focuses on understanding the underlying tasks or
'jobs' that customers need to accomplish."

**Attributed origin** [S110]: "popularized by Clayton Christensen, a Harvard Business School
professor... who applied it in the context of disruptive innovation," with later works
including *The Jobs to Be Done Playbook* by Jim Kalbach. [S054] additionally credits **Bob
Moesta**, CEO of the ReWired Group, as "an early pioneer of Jobs to be Done — as he
collaborated often with Clayton Christensen."

**How to apply** [S110]:
1. **Identify customer jobs** — in-depth interviews and observation focusing on "the context,
   triggers, and desired outcomes of these jobs rather than just the product features."
2. **Analyze and prioritize jobs** — by **how underserved they are** by current solutions and
   the potential impact of solving them.
3. **Design solutions around jobs** — and validate with customers.

**When it fits** [S110]: when teams "need to uncover the deeper motivations behind customer
behavior"; shifting "from feature-centric development to user-centric design"; industries
"where customers have complex needs that aren't fully addressed by existing solutions"; when
a team is "struggling to understand why customers use their product or how to differentiate."

## 4. Double Diamond

**What it is** [S110]: "a design framework that divides the creative process into four phases
— two diamonds representing divergent and convergent thinking — to guide teams from problem
discovery to solution delivery."

**Attributed origin:** [S110] states it "was developed by the Design Council, a UK-based
organization, in 2003." [S028] independently references "the double-diamond diagram introduced
by the UK Design Council" and cites "Design Council's evolved Double Diamond." Two independent
corpus sources agree on the originating organization.

**The four phases** [S110]:

| Diamond | Phase | Mode | Activity |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | **Discover** | Diverge | Research to understand the problem space — user interviews, market analysis, contextual insights, "without jumping to solutions" |
| First | **Define** | Converge | Synthesize findings to "narrow down and clearly define the core problem," ensuring the problem statement is "well-articulated and based on real user needs" |
| Second | **Develop** | Diverge | Ideation, sketching, prototyping — "explore different approaches and expand the range of possibilities" |
| Second | **Deliver** | Converge | Refine and test the most promising solutions, iterate on user-testing feedback, converge on the final product |

**When it fits** [S110]: "complex challenges that require thorough exploration before deciding
on a solution"; "when the problem space is unclear."

**Limitations / assumptions:**
- It is a **model of thinking modes**, not a project plan; it prescribes no durations,
  methods, or artefacts.
- It assumes the team is permitted to diverge before converging — the same organizational
  permission problem [S110] identifies as the reason discovery gets skipped.
- Real work rarely proceeds in one clean pass; the corpus does not address iteration between
  diamonds.

## 5. Lean Startup

**What it is** [S110]: "a methodology focused on building and launching products quickly
through iterative cycles of testing, learning, and adapting, with the goal of achieving a
sustainable business model based on validated customer feedback."

**Attributed origin** [S110]: "developed by Eric Ries and is based on principles from Lean
Manufacturing, Agile development, and Steve Blank's customer development process."
> Ries's *The Lean Startup* is **not in the corpus**. Attribution unverified against primary source.

**How to apply** [S110]:
1. **Build a Minimum Viable Product (MVP)** — "the simplest version of your product that can
   be released to early adopters," focused on "the core features that address the primary
   customer problem, allowing you to quickly gather feedback with minimal investment while
   testing product viability."
2. **Test and learn** — release to a small segment, gather feedback, "measure key metrics to
   test your hypotheses about the product, market, and customer behavior."
3. **Pivot or persevere** — pivot means "a fundamental change to the product or business
   strategy"; persevere means "continue refining and expanding." [S110] calls this decision
   "central to iterating towards a successful product-market fit."
4. **Balance innovation with feasibility** — avoid only incremental improvement, but filter
   radical ideas "through feasibility assessments."

**When it fits** [S110]: "high uncertainty or when resources are limited."

> **Important gap.** This passage is the corpus's **only substantive definition of MVP**, and
> it is a one-sentence, second-hand description inside a framework summary. There is no
> dedicated treatment of MVP anywhere in the corpus — no discussion of what "viable" means,
> how to scope an MVP, the common failure modes, or the alternatives (MLP, prototype, concierge
> MVP, Wizard of Oz). Given that "How is an MVP defined?" is a core question for a PM agent,
> this is a **high-priority research gap.** See [RESEARCH_BACKLOG.md](../RESEARCH_BACKLOG.md).
> [S090] separately mentions a "Minimum Lovable Product" without defining it against MVP.

## 6. Opportunity Solution Tree (OST)

**What it is** [S110]: "a visual framework that helps teams systematically explore, map out,
and prioritize opportunities to achieve a desired outcome. It links potential solutions
directly to customer needs and business goals."

**Attributed origin** [S110]: "developed by Teresa Torres, a product discovery coach, to
provide a structured way for teams to visualize and explore various opportunities... grounded
in user-centered design and continuous discovery practices."
> Torres's own writing is **not in the corpus.** [S041] separately quotes Torres as a
> "Product Discovery Coach" but does not cover the OST.

**How to build one** [S110]:
1. **Define the desired outcome** — "a business goal, such as increasing user retention, or a
   customer outcome, like improving user satisfaction. The outcome should be specific and
   measurable."
2. **Map opportunities** — research (user interviews, customer data analysis) to uncover
   "problems, pain points, or unmet needs that, if addressed, could lead to the desired
   outcome." Branch them from the central outcome. This step is **divergent**.
3. **Explore and prioritize solutions** — brainstorm solutions per opportunity, branching from
   each. Prioritize by "impact, feasibility, and alignment with strategic goals."
4. **Test and iterate** — using MVPs, prototypes, or experiments; refine based on results.

**When it fits** [S110]: "complex problems with multiple possible solutions"; organizations
focused on continuous discovery, "ensuring that the team's efforts are always aligned with key
objectives and avoid wasting resources on low-impact initiatives."

**Why the structure matters for reasoning:** the OST enforces that a *solution* is always
attached to an *opportunity*, which is always attached to an *outcome*. An idea with no
parent opportunity is, by construction, unjustified. This makes it the corpus's clearest
mechanism for connecting discovery to prioritization.

## Cross-cutting limitations

- **Every framework in this document is described second-hand.** [S110], a commercial training
  provider, is the sole source for five of the six. No primary text by Knapp, Ries, Torres,
  Sy, or the Design Council is in the corpus. Attributions and dates should be verified before
  being relied on.
- **No comparative evidence.** The corpus contains nothing measuring whether any of these
  frameworks improves outcomes, or under what conditions one outperforms another. Statements
  about "when it fits" are the source's judgment.
- **Framework stacking is not addressed.** In practice these combine (a Design Sprint *inside*
  a dual-track discovery, producing branches for an OST). The corpus does not discuss how.
- **[S110] omits well-known frameworks** including continuous discovery habits, the
  assumption/risk-mapping approaches referenced in [S028] and [S087], and Cagan's four product
  risks. Absence from this list is not evidence of unimportance.

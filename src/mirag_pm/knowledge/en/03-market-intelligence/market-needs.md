# Identifying Market Needs

## Definition

[S041]: "Market needs are essentially **the problems, desires, and gaps that exist in a target
market**; the things customers **want solved, improved, or fulfilled.**"

### Three kinds of need

[S041] separates them, and the distinction matters because products routinely address only the
first:

| Type | Definition [S041] | Examples given |
|---|---|---|
| **Functional** | "Practical problems people want to solve" | "Faster delivery, easier onboarding" |
| **Emotional** | "**How customers want to feel**" | "Secure using a banking app, proud wearing a brand" |
| **Social** | "**What helps them connect, belong, or gain status**" | "Platforms that build community, eco-friendly products that signal values" |

> This triad is the same one JTBD uses — [S054]: "the **functional, emotional, and social
> dimensions** that drive people to seek a solution" — and that [S005] requires in the Osterwalder
> canvas ("including emotional and social drivers, not just functional ones").
>
> **Three independent sources in this corpus converge on it.** That convergence is the strongest
> reason to treat it as reliable rather than as one framework's vocabulary. See
> [../02-discovery/jobs-to-be-done.md](../02-discovery/jobs-to-be-done.md).

### The three questions underneath
[S041]: "When businesses talk about 'identifying market needs,' they're really asking:
**What do people struggle with today? What's missing in their current options? What would make
them choose us over competitors?**"

## The premise

[S041] opens with the failure it is written against:

> "**Most market expansion efforts have the same story: the creators thought they understood the
> market needs, but reality told a different tale.** Maybe customers didn't use the product the way
> it was intended. **Maybe the 'pain point' wasn't painful enough.**"

> "*The pain point wasn't painful enough*" is a precise and useful formulation. A validated problem
> is not the same as a problem worth paying to solve — which is why [S092]'s question **"Can they
> avoid using our product?"** matters. See
> [../04-strategy/product-value.md](../04-strategy/product-value.md).

## Process

[S041] gives eight steps; the first three extracted in full.

### 1. Define your assumptions
"Begin with what you believe to be true. **What problem do you think exists, who experiences it, and
how might your solution fit in? Writing it down gives you a baseline to validate later.**"

See [../02-discovery/hypotheses-and-assumptions.md](../02-discovery/hypotheses-and-assumptions.md).

#### PESTLE analysis
[S041] recommends pairing assumptions with a **PESTLE** scan — "**political, economic, social,
technological, legal, and environmental** factors" — to "surface both **enablers and barriers**
that might affect your market."

Its worked prompts:

| Factor | Question [S041] |
|---|---|
| **Political** | "Are upcoming policies or trade restrictions going to impact your product?" |
| **Economic** | "Is the target audience's **purchasing power** growing or shrinking?" |
| **Social** | "Are there cultural shifts or lifestyle trends that increase demand?" |
| **Technological** | "What new tech could either **support or disrupt** your idea?" |
| **Environmental** | "Do sustainability concerns change how people will **perceive or use** your product?" |

*(Legal is named in the acronym; [S041]'s prompt for it did not extract.)*

> PESTLE is also named by [S030] as an initial-screening tool for ideas and opportunities. It is
> the corpus's only **macro-environment** framework, and it is the natural complement to
> competitive analysis, which looks only at the competitive set.

#### A question that avoids leading the witness
[S041]:

> "Instead of pitching your product right away, ask people: '**If you had a magic wand to make
> problems go away, what would you fix first?**'
>
> **If your idea naturally shows up in their answers, it's a strong signal you're addressing a real
> need rather than forcing a solution.**"

> The test is well-constructed: it makes the *absence* of your idea informative. [S044] (NN/g)
> independently recommends a near-identical discovery-survey question: "**If you had a magic wand,
> what would you change about [product/service]?**" See
> [../02-discovery/surveys.md](../02-discovery/surveys.md).

### 2. Perform a competitor analysis
"Look closely at competitor offerings, customer reviews, and feedback forums. You'll often find
gaps or frustrations that signal unmet needs."

**The specific technique** [S041]: "**A smart trick here is to scan competitors' negative reviews.
Customers will tell you exactly where current providers fall short.** If you can flip those
weaknesses into your strengths — faster service, better quality, a friendlier interface — you've
carved out your angle in a 'saturated' market."

> Corroborated by [S081] (mine G2/Trustpilot/Capterra for "recurring user complaints, gaps in
> current offerings, and unmet expectations") and [S025] ("customer reviews and community
> discussions often reveal recurring strengths and pain points that product pages do not
> highlight"). **Three sources converge on review mining as a high-yield, low-cost method.** See
> [competitive-analysis.md](competitive-analysis.md).

### 3. Talk to potential users
*(The section content did not extract.)* See
[../02-discovery/user-interviews.md](../02-discovery/user-interviews.md).

### Remaining steps
[S041]'s summary lists the full structured process as: "**assumption mapping, competitor analysis,
user interviews, quantifying problems, prototyping, and testing** — [this] helps turn ideas into
validated insights."

> **"Quantifying problems"** is the step most often skipped and is exactly what addresses the
> "pain point wasn't painful enough" failure above. The corpus does not explain how to do it.

## Segmentation

[S041]: "Market segmentation (**demographic, geographic, behavioral, psychographic**) ensures
solutions are tailored to the right audience."

Its named dimensions: "Demographic → Age, gender, income, education · Geographic → Location,
climate, city size · Behavioral → Buying habits, product usage, brand loyalty."

See [market-research.md](market-research.md).

## Cross-cultural and cross-market research

[S041] makes a point no other source in the corpus makes, and it is important for anyone expanding
a product:

> "**Cross-cultural research requires re-learning from scratch, respecting local behaviors,
> cultural norms, and regulations; assumptions from one market rarely translate directly to
> another.**"

> This is a strong claim — *re-learning from scratch* — and it directly contradicts the common
> assumption that a validated product needs only localisation to enter a new market. It is
> consistent with [S081]'s guidance that entering a new market or vertical warrants a full
> opportunity assessment, and with [S025]'s note that a new segment brings "different expectations,
> pricing sensitivities, and workflow preferences."

## Limitations

- **[S041] is a UX-tooling vendor's guide** (UXtweak) and each section ends by naming its own
  product features. The method content is separable and is retained; the tool claims are excluded.
- **Five of the eight process steps did not extract**, including "quantifying problems," which is
  the step the article's own premise most requires.
- **It cites "marketers in communities like r/marketing"** as a source for the PESTLE
  recommendation — a discovery-quality source, not an authority.
- **Teresa Torres is quoted** in [S041] as "Product Discovery Coach" but the quotation content did
  not extract.
- **[S009] is a low-reliability marketing-agency source** whose unverified statistics are
  quarantined in
  [../02-discovery/identifying-unmet-needs.md](../02-discovery/identifying-unmet-needs.md). Its
  techniques overlap with this document.
- **No treatment of how to size or quantify a need**, which is the gap between "a need exists" and
  "a market exists."

# The Product Lifecycle

## Origin

[S072] gives a specific and checkable attribution:

> "The concept of a product lifecycle gained critical attention in **1965 when economist Theodore
> Levitt published an influential article titled *Exploit the Product Life Cycle* in Harvard
> Business Review.** Levitt pointed out that although most business leaders were aware of the
> product lifecycle, **few were incorporating it into their product strategy or decision-making.
> Levitt noted that decisions made today should consider the next stage ahead.**"

> **This is one of very few citations in the corpus to a named, dated, peer-reviewed-adjacent
> publication.** The article itself is not in the corpus, so the attribution is second-hand — but it
> is specific enough to verify.
>
> **Levitt's actual point is the one most often lost:** the lifecycle is not a description to
> observe, it is **an input to present decisions.** Knowing which stage you are in tells you what
> to prepare for next.

[S072] adds that the idea "took hold in the 1980s," citing **American Motors Corporation (AMC)** as
"the first highly successful example. Facing competitors with larger budgets, AMC needed a way
to..." *(the account is truncated).*

## How many stages? — a real disagreement

The corpus does not agree on the number of stages, and the disagreement is substantive rather than
cosmetic.

| Source | Stages |
|---|---|
| **[S072]** (Aha!) | **Six:** Product development → Introduction → Growth → Maturity → **Saturation** → Decline |
| **[S071]** | **Four/five:** Introduction → Growth → Maturity → Decline |
| **[S118]** (ProductPlan) | Describes a progression without naming stages: pre-launch subject-matter expertise → **growth** → **retention** → **decline** → **end-of-life** |

[S072] addresses the disagreement directly: "**Some people lump saturation in with maturity. But
there are subtle differences that present unique challenges.**"

> **The saturation distinction is worth keeping**, because it names a state teams recognise but
> often misdiagnose: growth has stopped, the product is not declining, and *incremental improvement
> has stopped producing returns*. Treating saturation as maturity leads to more of the same
> feature work; treating it as decline leads to premature abandonment. See below.

## The six stages

### 1. Product development
[S072]: "encompasses everything from **early-stage ideation and research to planning and actually
building.** Typically, company leadership sets the strategic direction, including **a theory of how
a new offering will create value: the product vision.** This strategy is informed by market and
customer research."

**A contested claim about MVP** [S072]:

> "**For many years, the proverbial wisdom was that you need only to deliver a minimum viable
> product, getting to market as quickly as possible with as little as possible. But today's
> forward-thinking product teams focus on delivering a Minimum Lovable Product (MLP) — the minimum
> required for customers to love** [it]."

> ⚠ **MLP is Aha!'s own term and this is a marketing position, not an established shift.** The
> corpus contains **no definition of MLP** and no independent corroboration. It is recorded because
> it appears repeatedly in [S072] and [S090]'s navigation, and because it illustrates the corpus's
> broader weakness on MVP — see
> [../02-discovery/discovery-frameworks.md](../02-discovery/discovery-frameworks.md).

Named activities: "Prototyping and testing functionality · Developing an initial offering or MLP."

### 2. Introduction
[S072]: "centers around **the go-to-market strategy. This includes defining product positioning, a
promotion plan, and timing for a launch event.**"

The diagnostic function of this stage: "Product teams **gauge how well they assessed product-market
fit during the development stage** by analyzing early market and customer feedback. **The goal is to
identify and act on opportunities to refine** [the offering]."

"Some organizations choose a **beta program**, giving a small group of users early access in
exchange for detailed [feedback]."

Named activities: "Fixing bugs and enhancing functionality · **Prioritizing features on the product
roadmap**."

See [go-to-market.md](go-to-market.md) and [product-launch.md](product-launch.md).

### 3. Growth
[S072]: "when **customer demand for the product increases.** Users are becoming familiar with your
offering — folks are actively using and purchasing it... Momentum builds, and you have the
opportunity to deliver more value to more people."

**The competitive consequence** [S072]: "The growth stage is typically when **the market begins to
expand. Other companies might want to capture a share of it for themselves. Establishing your
product's place and demonstrating why it is the best solution becomes vitally** [important]."

Named activities: "**Scaling sales and support systems** · Soliciting customer success stories."

> Growth is where competitive analysis and positioning become urgent rather than preparatory. See
> [../03-market-intelligence/competitive-analysis.md](../03-market-intelligence/competitive-analysis.md)
> and [../04-strategy/positioning.md](../04-strategy/positioning.md).

### 4. Maturity
[S072]: "when **growth stabilizes.** Products in this stage are well known and loved by users.
**However, the return on advertising efforts often begins to diminish, and sales (although steady)
might soften. Competition is increasing, so maintaining market share is critical.**"

A recognisable signal: "You might see product messaging that includes claims of being the '**best**'
or '**most trusted**' solution during this stage."

**The strategic posture** [S072]: "**If you are fortunate, maturity is the longest of the six
stages.** Product teams that enjoy lasting success focus on **continual improvement — looking for
ways to cut costs, attract new users, reduce churn, and grow existing accounts.**"

See [managing-maturity.md](managing-maturity.md).

### 5. Saturation
[S072]: "a **plateau period. There is a glut of alternative solutions, and users might be more
interested in trying newer offerings over existing ones.**"

Its distinguishing difficulty: "the product team has likely released many different versions.
**Finding innovation opportunities (rather than rote enhancements to existing functionality) can be
difficult. As new customer acquisition slows, product development efforts often shift to retaining
existing customers.**"

**The strategic opening** [S072]: "**executive teams looking ahead might use this time to pivot and
transform — cycling back to the development stage.**"

> The loop back to development is the most important structural claim in [S072]'s model. **The
> lifecycle is not necessarily one-directional.** This is the same idea [S111] calls **PMF
> expansion** — "increasing the ceiling on PMF in a non-incremental way" — reached from a different
> tradition. See
> [../04-strategy/product-market-fit.md](../04-strategy/product-market-fit.md).

### 6. Decline
[S072]: "when **demand for the product begins to diminish.** There are a few reasons... **Competitors
could have outflanked you in pricing, services, or** [features]."

See [decline-and-end-of-life.md](decline-and-end-of-life.md).

## The PM's role changes by stage

[S118] describes the shift:

> "**Before launch, subject matter expertise was the priority** for product managers... they're
> prioritizing features for the MVP to make sure they're delivering something with real benefits to
> the market. **After the initial launch, the focus quickly shifts to growth.** Product management
> worries about scale while adding functionality that continues to propel growth. **Once all those
> new users are onboard, the emphasis transitions to retention.** It's all about what's required to
> keep customers happy and minimize churn. **Most products inevitably begin to decline in usage**,
> which presents new challenges. Product managers must consider **how the product can be
> repurposed, extended, or pivoted toward new verticals.**"

> [S075] (ProductPlan, *Product Management's Role at Every Phase of the Product Lifecycle*) covers
> the same ground; its content did not extract substantively.

## Why this framework matters for decisions

Levitt's original argument, per [S072] — **"decisions made today should consider the next stage
ahead"** — is what makes the lifecycle actionable rather than descriptive. Concretely:

| If you are in… | The decision-relevant question is… |
|---|---|
| **Development** | What will the introduction stage need that we must build now? |
| **Introduction** | Is PMF confirmed, or is the early feedback telling us it is not? |
| **Growth** | Can our systems and support scale, and is our position defensible as competitors enter? |
| **Maturity** | Where is the continual improvement coming from — cost, acquisition, churn, or expansion? |
| **Saturation** | Is incremental work still returning, or is this the moment to pivot back to development? |
| **Decline** | Is this reversible, and if not, what is the responsible exit? |

> This table is a **synthesis** of [S072]'s stage descriptions, not a corpus claim.

## Limitations

- **Stage count is disputed** (see above), and no source explains how to tell which stage a product
  is actually in — except in decline, where [S071] and [S134] give signals. **Stage identification
  is the hardest and least-covered part of the model.**
- **[S072] is a vendor guide** (Aha!) and its MLP claim is self-promotional.
- **Levitt's article is not in the corpus.** The attribution is second-hand.
- **The model assumes a market-facing product with a sales motion.** Its applicability to internal
  platforms, infrastructure, or free products is not addressed.
- **No treatment of how long stages last**, or of products that never leave one stage.
- **No engagement with criticism of the PLC model**, which is substantial in the marketing
  literature — notably that it is descriptive rather than predictive and risks becoming a
  self-fulfilling prophecy (a product declared to be in decline stops receiving investment and
  therefore declines).
- The **AMC example did not extract** beyond its opening.

# Managing a Mature Product

## The situation

[S072]: maturity is "when **growth stabilizes.** Products in this stage are well known and loved by
users. However, **the return on advertising efforts often begins to diminish, and sales (although
steady) might soften. Competition is increasing, so maintaining market share is critical.**"

Its strategic posture: "**If you are fortunate, maturity is the longest of the six stages.** Product
teams that enjoy lasting success focus on **continual improvement — looking for ways to cut costs,
attract new users, reduce churn, and grow existing accounts.**"

## Two pathologies of mature products

[S042] (Janna Bastow, ProdPad) names two failure modes specific to this stage. They are the most
valuable content on the topic.

### The feature factory

> "**Businesses want repeatable, consistent growth. Tons of features won't get them there.**
>
> In particular, **mature companies hold on to the cadence of 'launch, launch, launch' — once
> useful in the introduction and even growth stages — and start measuring the wrong things. They
> incentivize based on delivery velocity, burndown, story points, and volume.**" [S042]

> **The diagnosis is precise: the behaviour that worked in an earlier stage persists past its
> usefulness, and the measurement system reinforces it.** Velocity and story points are output
> measures; in maturity, the returns come from outcomes — churn, cost, expansion — which those
> measures cannot see.
>
> This connects directly to the outputs-versus-outcomes argument in
> [../04-strategy/okrs.md](../04-strategy/okrs.md), and to [S031]'s finding that most shipped
> features go unused — see
> [../05-planning/prioritization-frameworks.md](../05-planning/prioritization-frameworks.md).

### The backlog beast

> "Mature companies often end up with **a vertigo-inducing product backlog: a really long list of
> everything the team could possibly do.** This is **partially because of the feature factory
> pitfall, and partially because they don't continue** [applying product management best
> practices] — [the backlog] **becomes a repository** [for everything]." [S042]

> The causal claim is worth noting: **the backlog beast is a symptom of the feature factory**, not
> an independent problem. Pruning the backlog without changing what the team is measured on
> regenerates it. See
> [../05-planning/backlog-management.md](../05-planning/backlog-management.md).

[S042] also names "**risk of disruption**" alongside these two.

## The response: disrupt yourself

[S042]: "**If the threat of disruption is all around, then the key to survival is to disrupt
yourself!**" It gives four mechanisms for building experimentation into a mature organization.

### 1. Give the team time for R&D
> "Research and development are necessary to innovate your product. **Google allocates 20% of its
> time to it. Google Maps and Google Chat came out of this!** Find a percentage that makes sense for
> your business. **Whatever you can afford, give teams the space, time, and permission to think
> about things that might sit outside of your regular product box.**" [S042]

> ⚠ **The Google 20% claim is widely repeated and contested.** The corpus provides no source, and
> the attribution of Google Maps to 20% time is disputed in public accounts. **Do not cite this as
> fact.** Recorded in
> [../99-reference/disputed-information.md](../99-reference/disputed-information.md).
>
> The underlying principle — *allocate protected, permissioned time outside the roadmap* — does not
> depend on the example.

### 2. Hackathon
> "Hackathons are **as good for building interesting new product areas as they are for building
> teams and cohesion. And any team can do one! It could just be a day, it could be a week.**" [S042]

### 3. The Lab
> "'The Lab' is **creating an innovation hub within a larger organization, whose task it is to
> discover and internally 'disrupt' the company. It's like running a startup in-house or having an
> R&D department. This is pretty popular with big corporations, banks, and big tech.**" [S042]

[S042] points to **Clayton Christensen's *The Innovator's Dilemma*** for more on this — **not in
the corpus.**

> The Lab corresponds to [S111]'s **Innovation PM** specialization: "part of a new skunkworks or
> innovation based team at a growing/established org." See
> [../01-foundations/pm-specializations.md](../01-foundations/pm-specializations.md).

### 4. Hyper-segmented rollouts
[S042] describes a practice attributed to Facebook: "**They use hyper-segmentation. With the
world's largest user base, they never launch anything to everyone all at once. Instead, they segment
to a fine degree, targeting only the most relevant users.**"

> This is the only staged-rollout practice described anywhere in the corpus. It is framed here as
> an *innovation-enabling* mechanism rather than a risk-reduction one — it lowers the cost of
> trying something, which is what makes experimentation affordable at scale. Compare [S031]'s
> **reversibility** criterion in
> [../05-planning/prioritization-frameworks.md](../05-planning/prioritization-frameworks.md).

## What maturity-stage work looks like

Combining [S072] and [S042], the shift is **from adding to optimising**:

| Away from | Toward |
|---|---|
| Launch cadence and feature volume | Churn reduction, cost efficiency, account expansion |
| Velocity, burndown, story points | Retention, unit economics, outcome measures |
| Backlog accumulation | Deliberate pruning and prioritization against current goals |
| Incremental enhancement only | Protected capacity for exploration |

[S072]'s named maturity activities are consistent: "cut costs, attract new users, reduce churn, and
grow existing accounts."

## When maturity becomes saturation

[S072] distinguishes them: in saturation, "**Finding innovation opportunities (rather than rote
enhancements to existing functionality) can be difficult**," and "executive teams looking ahead might
use this time to **pivot and transform — cycling back to the development stage.**"

> The self-disruption practices above are how a team *prepares* for that transition before it is
> forced. A mature product with no exploration capacity arrives at saturation with nothing in the
> pipeline. See [product-lifecycle.md](product-lifecycle.md).

## Limitations

- **[S042] is a practitioner blog post** by a product-tool CEO; its claims are experiential and its
  examples (Google 20%, Facebook segmentation) are **unverified industry lore.**
- **No evidence** that any of the four self-disruption mechanisms works, or under what conditions.
- **No guidance on how much capacity to allocate** to exploration beyond "whatever you can afford."
- **No treatment of portfolio management** — how a mature product's resourcing is decided relative
  to newer products in the same company. [S134] touches on this from the decline side.
- **No treatment of pricing or packaging changes in maturity**, which is a standard lever and is
  absent from the corpus entirely.
- Christensen's *The Innovator's Dilemma* is referenced but not in the corpus.

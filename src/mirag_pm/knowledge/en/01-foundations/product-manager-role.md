# Product Manager Role and Responsibilities

## Definition

"A product manager is the person who drives the development of a product, defining its
strategy, roadmap, and features. They are responsible for identifying customers' needs and
the larger business objectives that a product or feature will fulfill, articulating what
success looks like for a product, and rallying a team to turn that vision into a reality."
[S078]

[S077] states it in market terms: "The Product Manager is responsible for delivering a
differentiated product to market that addresses a market need and represents a viable
business opportunity," with a key component being "ensuring that the product supports the
company's overall strategy and goals."

## The three pillars

[S140] (Martin Eriksson, founder of ProductTank) defines product management as "the
intersection between business, technology, and user experience," and states the competence
bar directly:

> "A good product manager must be experienced in at least one, passionate about all three,
> and conversant with practitioners in all." [S140]

| Pillar | What [S140] says it requires |
|---|---|
| **Business** | Product management "is above all else a business function, focused on maximising business value from a product." PMs should optimise for business goals while maximising return on investment. |
| **Technology** | "There's no point defining what to build if you don't know how it will get built." The PM need not code, but understanding the stack and level of effort is "crucial to making the right decisions" — more so in Agile, where PMs spend more daily time with developers than with anyone else in the business. |
| **User Experience** | The PM is "the voice of the user inside the business." Not a "pixel pusher," but must test the product, talk to users, and get feedback first-hand — "especially in a start-up." |

## The "CEO of the product" analogy and its limits

[S078] reports that Ben Horowitz called the product manager "the CEO of the product," and
that Eriksson's Venn diagram is widely cited — then argues **both are commonly
misinterpreted**:

- People read Eriksson's diagram as meaning PMs *manage* the product across UX, technology
  and business. [S078] says the actual claim is that PMs must **balance all three needs and
  make hard trade-offs**.
- People read Horowitz's analogy as implying PMs have special authority. [S078] states
  plainly: **"They don't."** The valid part of the analogy is that, like a CEO, PMs "set
  the goals, define success, help motivate teams, and are responsible for the outcome."

This distinction matters for an agent reasoning about the role: **the PM owns outcomes
without owning authority.** Decision rights must be earned or negotiated, not assumed.

## Core responsibilities

[S078] lists the tasks a good PM spends time on:

- Understanding and representing user needs
- Monitoring the market and developing competitive analyses
- Defining a vision for the product
- Aligning stakeholders around that vision
- Prioritizing product features and capabilities
- Creating "a shared brain" across larger teams to empower independent decision-making

[S077] gives a longer job-description-style list, which overlaps but adds commercial duties:

- Defines the product vision, strategy, and roadmap
- Gathers, manages, and prioritizes market/customer requirements
- Acts as the customer advocate, articulating the user's and/or buyer's needs
- Works closely with engineering, sales, marketing, and support so that business-case and
  customer-satisfaction goals are met
- Holds technical product knowledge or specific domain expertise
- Defines what to solve in a market requirements document
- Runs beta and pilot programs during the qualify phase
- Is a market expert: why customers buy, who the competition is, how customers think about
  and buy the product
- Develops the business case for new products and improvements
- Develops positioning for the product
- Recommends or contributes to product pricing (noted as **not true in all industries** —
  [S077] names insurance as an exception)

## How responsibilities vary with organization size

[S078]:

- **Larger organizations** — PMs are embedded within teams of specialists. Researchers,
  analysts and marketers gather input; developers and designers handle day-to-day
  execution. These PMs have more help but "spend more time aligning these stakeholders
  behind a specific vision."
- **Smaller organizations** — PMs "spend less time getting everyone to agree, but more time
  doing the hands-on work that comes with defining a vision and seeing it through."

## Typical deliverables

[S077] lists the documents PMs are commonly asked to produce, with the caveat that "each
company has their own specific list and terminology":

- Business case
- Market needs document
- Product roadmaps
- White papers, case studies, product comparisons, competitor analysis, user stories
- Presentations built on the above
- Spreadsheets documenting the underlying data

## Decision considerations: practices named as important

These are presented in [S078] as craft advice from a practising PM, not as validated
findings.

### Prioritize ruthlessly
[S078] likens the PM to a politician: both get an allotted amount of resources and must use
them toward a larger goal "knowing that he or she will never be able to satisfy everyone's
needs." Example trade-offs given: a feature that pleases one big customer but upsets 100
smaller ones; maintaining status quo versus expanding reach; "the bright and shiny" versus
"the boring and important."

### Know the lay of the land before executing
"Product managers very rarely start with a clean slate... If they start executing without
taking the time to get their bearings, they'll make bad decisions." The advice is to spend
the first couple of months talking to customers and internal stakeholders and understanding
the business model, the history, how people are influenced, and how decisions are made.

### Empower the team to decide
"Touching every decision isn't the product manager's job." [S078] frames the mechanism as
building a *shared brain* — a way of making decisions plus criteria for escalating them —
and offers a diagnostic: "When someone asks a product manager a question about a decision
they could have made themselves, nine times out of 10 it's because that person doesn't have
enough context."

### Influence without authority
[S078] and [S077] both name this. [S077] lists "proven ability to influence cross-functional
teams without formal authority" among hiring criteria. [S078] describes it as understanding
which levers work with which person, and notes storytelling matters "even when you don't
have any data to back up your point."

### Develop a thick skin
"Making tradeoffs will inevitably make people unhappy. The trick is to first make the right
tradeoffs, and then be able to explain why you made the decision you did."

## A caution about the "great PM" narrative

[S078] names Steve Jobs and Elon Musk as examples of visionary product thinkers, then
immediately qualifies it:

> "We idolize these people, in part, because it's satisfying to put a face and a name on a
> big accomplishment. But 99 percent of the time, great products aren't made by a single
> great thinker. They're made by teams of good people doing really good work." [S078]

## Limitations

- Every source here is a vendor blog, training provider, or practitioner essay. None is an
  empirical study of what PMs actually do.
- The responsibility lists conflict at the edges — for example, whether the PM or a project
  manager tracks delivery (see [product-vs-project-management.md](product-vs-project-management.md)),
  and whether the PM or UX owns discovery and ideation
  (see [pm-and-ux-collaboration.md](pm-and-ux-collaboration.md), which has survey evidence
  that practitioners themselves disagree).
- [S077] carries an "Updated: 11/02/20" stamp with an ambiguous date format; its currency
  cannot be determined.

# Product Management

## Definition

Product management is the organizational function responsible for the overall success
of a product. The corpus offers several formulations that agree on substance while
differing in emphasis:

- "The practice of strategically driving the development, market launch, and continual
  support and improvement of a company's products." [S118]
- "The practice of guiding a solution from a raw concept to market success. It requires
  balancing technical feasibility, business viability, and user value." [S132]
- "A multifaceted discipline that revolves around the conception, development, and
  marketing of a product throughout its lifecycle." [S122]
- "The function within an organization that's responsible for the overall success of a
  product. It sits at the intersection of business, UX, and technology." [S109]

All four sources converge on three constants: **a product-level (not project-level) scope**,
**a full-lifecycle span**, and **a balancing of business, technology and user dimensions**.

## What the discipline is responsible for

Across [S118], [S132] and [S122], the recurring responsibilities are:

- **Research** — building expertise about the market, user personas, and competitors.
- **Strategy** — turning that knowledge into a high-level plan with goals and objectives.
- **Communication** — expressing the plan (typically via a roadmap) to executives,
  investors, and development teams, and maintaining that alignment over time.
- **Coordination of development** — working with the teams that execute the plan.
- **Acting on feedback and data** — learning post-launch what works and feeding it back
  into future iterations.
- **Driving adoption and revenue** — investigating why a shipped feature is or is not
  adopted, and what is needed to make it succeed commercially. [S118]

## Why the definition varies between organizations

[S118] states explicitly that "the role itself means very different things in different
organizations," and [S078] attributes the confusion to the recency of the title: while the
function has existed "in one form or another for a number of decades," the *product
manager* title "only started picking up steam less than 20 years ago" (written by an
Atlassian PM; the article is undated in the corpus, so the anchor year cannot be fixed).

This variability is a structural property of the discipline, not a defect in any single
definition. See [pm-specializations.md](pm-specializations.md) for the argument that the
absence of formal specializations causes measurable organizational problems.

## Origin (as reported in the corpus)

[S132] traces product management to "the early 1930s at Procter & Gamble (P&G), where the
concept of 'brand management' was first introduced," with managers assigned to oversee
individual brands and act as the voice of the customer internally, later expanding beyond
marketing to cover the product lifecycle.

> **Verification status: UNVERIFIED.** This origin story appears in only one corpus source,
> a commercial training provider's blog, with no citation to a primary source. The claim is
> widely repeated in the industry but the corpus contains no primary documentation for it.
> See [RESEARCH_BACKLOG.md](../RESEARCH_BACKLOG.md).

## The strategic / tactical split

[S118] argues that product management is fundamentally a *strategic* function, and that
product managers "shouldn't be responsible for the ground-level details of the development
process." It reports that "innovative organizations separate this function and assign
tactical elements to project managers, such as scheduling and managing workloads."

This is presented by [S118] as a recommended organizational design, not as an observed
universal. It is in tension with [S078], which describes PMs at smaller organizations as
spending "more time doing the hands-on work." The two are reconcilable by company size, but
the corpus does not state this reconciliation explicitly.

## Common practice: a product management process

[S118] lists a ten-step sequence used to develop product strategy. It is presented as one
organization's articulation, not as a standard:

1. Talk to customers and prospects
2. Survey the competitive and market landscape
3. Develop and test a vision, including gathering cross-functional feedback
4. Define commercial goals and KPIs
5. Scope requirements
6. Add the product to the roadmap
7. Plan for deployment, including core messaging and positioning with GTM teams
8. Deploy and drive adoption
9. Measure performance and product acceptance
10. Optimize and iterate

## Value, usability, feasibility

[S122] states that the features a team focuses on should be **valuable** (solves a need
someone has), **usable** (allows long-term use without frustration), and **feasible**
(does not require excessive time, money, or effort). [S140] attributes a near-identical
formulation to Marty Cagan's book *Inspired*: the product manager's job is "to discover a
product that is valuable, usable and feasible."

See also the four-part desirable/viable/feasible framing in
[../12-design-and-ux/product-triad.md](../12-design-and-ux/product-triad.md), which the
corpus reports as having a different origin and a fourth dimension.

## Role levels and career trajectory

[S118] describes the following progression, noting that titles vary by company size:

| Level | Scope as described |
|---|---|
| Associate / Junior PM | New to the domain, more limited responsibilities |
| Product Manager | Owns one or more products, or a horizontal function across products |
| Senior PM | Broader scope |
| Technical PM | Often transitioning from engineering/IT; manages aspects requiring deeper technical understanding (infrastructure, APIs) |
| Product Line Manager | Owns multiple related products, may oversee individual-contributor PMs |
| Director / Senior Director | May be a title for a lone product professional, or indicate a broad portfolio |
| VP / SVP Product | Responsibility for more people, possibly including business analysts, UX, product marketing |
| CPO | Elevates product management to the C-suite; provides directional guidance and coaching rather than product detail |

[S118] adds that a CPO role "often indicates an organization is committed to product-led
growth." This is an interpretive claim by a roadmapping vendor, not evidence.

## B2B versus B2C product management

[S118] addresses a "common belief" that there is a vast difference between B2B and B2C
product management, and argues the differences are narrower than assumed:

- **B2C**: users are generally also the buyers; typically a single persona.
- **B2B**: the person controlling the budget is often unrelated to the person who uses the
  product, so each persona must be identified and addressed separately.
- **Both** require multiple value propositions, because even individual consumers look for
  several reasons to buy — practical, emotional, and financial.
- B2B sales require more convincing across more stakeholders; acquisition cost is higher
  and growth rate slower.

[S118]'s conclusion — that skills transfer between the two and PMs "shouldn't feel
pigeonholed" — is the author's position, not a documented finding.

## Limitations of the knowledge in this document

- No source in the corpus is a professional standards body or academic publication. All
  definitions here come from vendor blogs, training providers, or practitioner essays.
- The P&G origin claim is unverified (see above).
- The corpus contains no definition of product management from a body such as PMI,
  ISPMA, or an academic text. This is recorded as a gap.

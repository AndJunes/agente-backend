# Product Management Specializations and Types of Product Work

## The claim

[S111] (Reforge, 31 Aug 2021) argues that Design, Engineering, Marketing and Sales have each
had recognised specialties for years, while **product management has not formally codified
its own** — and that this absence causes concrete, nameable problems.

Its illustration of specialization elsewhere:
- Design → Product Design, Brand Design, UI/UX, Research, Interaction Design, Service Design
- Engineering → Frontend, Backend, Mobile (iOS/Android), Web, Full stack, Payments
- Marketing → Brand, Content, Performance marketing, SEO, CRM/Lifecycle, Marketing Ops, PR, Product Marketing
- Sales → Inbound, Outbound, SDR/BDR, AE, Technical Sales, CSM

## Problems caused by assuming all PM roles are equivalent

[S111] names five. Each is a *diagnostic pattern* an agent can match against a described
situation.

| Problem | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Tools don't transfer** | PMs apply the same tool to all situations, expecting success in one area to translate. Leads to "hitting a career progression ceiling because they don't know when to pull in other resources or take a totally different approach." |
| **Slow & steady struggle** | In an unfamiliar product area the PM "can't definitively decide on team direction, don't provide valuable impact to the org, and begin to doubt their own Product Management skills." |
| **Focus fatigue** | PM learning slows because they don't know where to focus — e.g. incentivised to "create a strategic vision" for promotion "but in reality the team needs a detailed week-by-week roadmap." |
| **Talent turmoil** | A company hires a strong PM from elsewhere "without vetting for specific areas of expertise," then can't explain why the hire isn't working. |
| **Competing colleagues** | PMs with different strengths are compared unfairly against each other, e.g. in bonus decisions. |

Worked example given for *tools don't transfer*: a PM with a payments-infrastructure
background at Etsy is asked to open a new product/revenue area with a small team. They
gravitate to setting up payment infrastructure, rather than "market research, user
interaction with the product, product market fit, or modeling addressable revenue impact."

## Baseline skills assumed of any PM

[S111] states that regardless of specialization, some skills are assumed:

- **Technical & data comfort** — analysis, interpreting dashboards, understanding or setting
  metrics/financial goals, working with developers and data science
- **Communication & collaboration** — motivating and leading a cross-functional team toward
  a goal while getting buy-in from others
- **Problem solving** — breaking down an ambiguous product/customer need and experimenting
  or iterating toward a solution
- **User understanding** — empathizing, listening, co-creating with the end customer
- **Strategic thinking** — recentering on larger organization, user, or investor goals while
  accounting for many moving parts

## The four types of product work

This is the analytical core of the framework. [S111] attributes it to Fareed Mosavat
(VP Programs/Partners at Reforge, ex-Director of Product at Slack) and Casey Winters
(CPO at Eventbrite, ex-Product Growth Lead at Pinterest), from their piece *Product Work
Beyond Product-Market Fit*.

Their stated problem: "Most PMs are unaware of the different types of product work and which
one is most relevant to them for what they are working on. This makes it difficult for them
to be effective in their role, as they are using a sub-optimal or incorrect method
(hammer/nail problem)." They add that "PMs tend to believe what previously worked for them or
the organization in the past will work again in the future."

| Type of work | What it does | When it applies |
|---|---|---|
| **Feature work** | "Creates and captures value by extending a product's functionality and market into incremental and adjacent areas." Where most PMs start. | Once a product reaches initial product-market fit |
| **Growth work** | "Creates and captures value by capturing more of the existing market." Connects customers to value that already exists rather than creating new value. The prompting question: "How are users going to find out about and build a habit around this feature?" | Alongside/before feature build-out |
| **Scaling work** | Addresses "new bottlenecks [that] occur that slow down or inhibit the team's ability to ship new features and move quickly." Ensures the team retains the ability to ship across all other work types. | As a product accelerates its growth |
| **Product-market fit expansion** | "Increasing the ceiling on PMF in a non-incremental way to expand into an adjacent market, adjacent product, or both." | When growth slows as the product "hits some type of saturation" |

### The depth-before-breadth claim
[S111]: "a Product Manager should master 1 to 2 areas of product work at best... depth in one
type of product work should eventually translate to breadth across multiple types."

> **Classification: practitioner position.** Presented without supporting evidence.

## The four specializations

Each type of work maps to a specialization.

### Core PM (feature work)
- **Focus:** "laser focused on solving for a customer pain point or need."
- **Common areas:** search, community/social, international, newsfeed, and other
  customer-facing experiences.
- **Other names / sub-specialities:** Feature PM, UX PM, core consumer PM, enterprise PM,
  community PM, international PM.
- **Key skills:** user-centered design, holistic product thinking, problem- and
  hypothesis-centered work, end-to-end user experience, user empathy, research.
- **Extra-close collaborators:** Research, Design, Support.

### Growth PM (growth work)
- **Focus:** the customer's journey "through the lens of business metrics like acquisition,
  CAC, sign ups, free trial starts, conversion/purchase rates, monetization, ARPU, and
  retention."
- **Surface area:** sign-up/registration, onboarding, conversion/monetization, pricing,
  referrals/sharing, retention/transaction moments, revenue growth.
- **Other names:** Activation PM, Engagement PM, Retention PM, Monetization PM, Conversion
  PM, Growth infra PM.
- **Key skills:** experimentation, optimization, speed to deploy, marketing skills, financial
  and experimentation modeling, quick decision making, channel skills such as SEO, pricing
  philosophy, "solid data and statistics skills," iterative development.
- **Extra-close collaborators:** Finance, Data, Marketing.

### Platform PM (scaling work)
- **Focus:** internal customers and needs; "scaling internal platforms and services for
  continued organizational growth." Typically the customers are **internal stakeholders and
  other product teams**, not external users.
- **Scope examples:** trust and safety, security, moderation, support and customer
  experience, internal data and analytics, machine learning, payments and billing,
  authentication/identity, personalization.
- **Other names:** Tech PM, Data PM, Infra PM, Security PM, Identity PM, Internal Tools PM,
  Personalization PM.
- **Key skills:** scaled development thinking, technical/engineering/data thinking, comfort
  with the tech stack, ability to bridge tech and business stakeholders, **build-vs-buy
  decision making**, internal stakeholder management, efficiency, service design.
- **Extra-close collaborators:** Data/Data Science, Customer Support, Legal,
  Finance/Accounting.
- **Observed signals** (from Crystal Widjaja, CPO at Kumu, ex-SVP Growth and BI at Gojek):
  a PM suited to infrastructure work "liked reading documentation of APIs, they were excited
  to figure out bugs and finding small optimizations within the services, and they cared a lot
  about quality of our services" — in contrast to a visionary ecommerce role with "huge design
  bets" they were struggling with.

### Innovation PM (product-market fit expansion)
- **Focus:** "identifying and experimenting with new opportunities to reach and expand
  product market fit" — exploring adjacent users, new verticals, net-new products, novel
  revenue streams.
- **Where they sit:** typically (1) the first product hire or co-founder driving initial PMF,
  or (2) part of a skunkworks/innovation team at a growing or established organization.
- **Other names:** Expansion PM, 0→1 PM, New Verticals PM, Skunkworks/Stealth PM, Third
  Horizon PM, R&D PM, Special Projects PM.
- **Key skills:** 0-1 thinking, "extreme comfort w/ ambiguity," reference customer
  development, strong visioning, "PMF discovery and ability to pivot without attachment to
  ideas," fundraising, storytelling to execs / investors / board.
- **Extra-close collaborators:** Executive team, Research.

## Evidence quality of this framework

[S111] supports the taxonomy with **real job descriptions** copied from company career pages
in August 2021 — Square, WhatsApp and Stripe for Core PM; Zillow, Slack and ZipRecruiter for
Growth PM; Affirm, Strava and Spotify for Platform PM; Airbnb, DoorDash and Netflix for
Innovation PM. This is unusually concrete grounding for a practitioner framework: the
categories are shown to correspond to roles that actually existed.

It also carries first-person accounts from named practitioners with verifiable roles
(Adam Fishman, ex-VP Product at Patreon; Keya Patel, ex-Product Growth at Dropbox; Crystal
Widjaja, CPO at Kumu).

## Limitations, assumptions and when this may not apply

- **The job-description evidence is dated August 2021.** Role titles and scopes in this
  industry change; the specific postings are historical.
- **The framework assumes a company large enough to specialize.** In a startup or a team
  with one PM, all four types of work land on the same person; the framework then functions
  as a checklist of modes rather than as a staffing model.
- **It assumes the product has or is pursuing product-market fit.** Feature, growth and
  scaling work are all defined relative to PMF.
- **Reforge is a commercial education provider** and the article repeatedly points to its own
  programs. The taxonomy is nonetheless substantive and separable from the promotion.
- **The taxonomy is not authoritative.** [S111] itself says specializations "haven't been
  formally codified" — this is a proposal, not a standard. [S118] independently lists a
  different cut of specializations (product research, product design, product data analysis,
  product operations, product strategy, product marketing), showing that even within the
  corpus there is no single accepted breakdown.
- [S111] notes sub-specialization was already emerging within these categories (it raises
  pricing PMs and payments PMs as candidates) without resolving it.
